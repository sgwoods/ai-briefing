Deep dive: One Point Apart: What a Near-Tie on an AI Index Hides

AI Briefing · 2026-11-15 · follows today's briefing · written and read by AI from public sources

Google's Gemini 4 Hydrogen scored within a point of Claude Sonnet 5.5 and GPT-6.1 Sol on Artificial Analysis' Intelligence Index, at about a third of Sonnet's cost per task. We explain what the composite index averages, why a one-point gap can still hide a real difference on long agent work, and what founders and investors might check before pricing a vendor switch on that one number.

Why today

Google released Gemini 4 Hydrogen this week. It is the cheapest member of the Gemini 4 family. Within hours, Artificial Analysis, an independent benchmarking firm, placed it just below Claude Sonnet 5.5 and GPT-6.1 Sol on its Intelligence Index. At its highest reasoning setting, Hydrogen scored 54. Both rivals scored 55. Artificial Analysis put Hydrogen's cost at about a third of Sonnet's. Artificial Analysis also put it at roughly 2.5x the cost per task of GPT-6.1 Sol at max reasoning, even as it came in at about a third of Claude Sonnet 5.5's cost. Hydrogen is also Google's new free-tier model, and it will power AI features in Google Search. Procurement teams and founders will be tempted to reprice vendors on that single number. A one-point gap sounds like nothing. Whether it really is nothing depends on what the index averages, and on which of those tasks look like your own workload. So let's open the index up.

Plainly

The Intelligence Index is a composite. Artificial Analysis runs nine separate tests and combines them as a weighted average. There are four categories, each worth a quarter of the score: knowledge work, coding, science, and general knowledge. Inside those categories the weights vary. A test of multi-week business projects, called AA-Briefcase, carries 15%. A test of real code changes in open-source repositories carries another 15%. Terminal-Bench, where the model works at a command line to finish multi-step tasks, carries 10%. A knowledge test that also penalises made-up answers carries 15%. Here is the analogy worth keeping. Think of a decathlon. Ten events, points added up, one total. Two athletes can finish a point apart while one of them is a weak pole vaulter. If you are hiring a pole vaulter, the total misleads you. Now the arithmetic, using a real gap. An independent test by Artificial Analysis found Hydrogen lags on terminal work, and Google's own results put it at 57.0% on Terminal-Bench 4.1, against 63.9% for Claude Sonnet 5.5. That test is worth a tenth of the index. So a ten-point gap there is worth about one index point by itself. That is roughly the entire headline difference. Which tells you Hydrogen must be level or ahead elsewhere to finish that close. A near-tie in total can be a real gap in one event plus wins in others. Why does that matter for agents? Because long tasks compound errors. Take an illustrative case, not a measured one. A model that slips on one step in fifty will usually hit at least one slip over a hundred steps. Small differences per step become large differences in finished jobs. One more trap: price per token is not price per task. At its top setting, Hydrogen produced 304M output tokens running the index. The median model produced 77.3M. A cheap token used four times over is not cheap. That is why the per-task cost is the number to watch.

What the evidence shows

Start with what an independent party measured. Artificial Analysis ran Hydrogen through its own harness at four reasoning levels. It scored 54 at the highest, 51 at the default, then 41 and 27 at lower settings. Artificial Analysis estimates $3.16 per task at the top level and $1.87 at default. That is consistent with the one-third claim. On speed, Gemini 4 Hydrogen at its Xhigh setting took about 14.7 minutes per Intelligence Index task, which is slower than the 13.2-minute median for comparable models. Artificial Analysis measured an 8% hallucination rate on its knowledge test. Simon Willison, an independent developer, published the token prices from hands-on use. Hydrogen is $1.50 per million input tokens and $7.50 per million output. He notes it is 15x the price of Claude Haiku 5.5 and GPT-6 Luna. His informal drawing test found the quality drop from its bigger siblings, in his words, "quite stark." Now what is self-reported. Google's model card claims Hydrogen beats Sonnet 5.5 on a bug-finding test, 13.5% against 3.1%. It claims a lead on SaaS workflow automation, 76.1% against 71.8%. To its credit, the same card concedes Sonnet leads on Terminal-Bench 4.1, 63.9% against 57.0%. That comes through launch coverage, on a test nobody outside Box can inspect. Google's Robby Stein called it "the biggest model upgrade to Search yet." That is an executive describing his own product. What is not shown yet is the thing buyers care most about. We found no independent, head-to-head test of Hydrogen against Sonnet on long, real production agent runs. One widely shared post said Hydrogen "blows away" Sonnet 5.5. The index it cites says the opposite.

How people read it

The same facts support different readings. The AGI-forward reading sees speed. A cheap tier now sits within a point of the frontier on an index built from hard work, including multi-week business projects. Capability is moving down the price ladder in weeks, not years. On that view, the remaining gaps will close with the next release, and betting against cheap models is betting against the trend. The AI-realist reading looks at where the gap sits. It is concentrated on long command-line work, the kind of chained task agents actually do. Averages smooth exactly that away. Labs also tune for well-known benchmarks, and Hydrogen's heavy token use suggests some of its score is bought with extra thinking. The market reading looks at price per finished task. By Artificial Analysis' own numbers, GPT-6.1 Sol scores higher and costs less per task. So Hydrogen is not the cheapest near-frontier option; it is cheaper than Sonnet. Google's pricing also looks provisional. Its bigger Carbon and Argon models double in price on December 1. Hydrogen goes from a 25% discount on them to a 75% discount. Google can also spread costs across Search, which a standalone lab cannot. Pricing set for strategy can change when the strategy does.

What it means for builders and investors

Our read: a composite index is a good screening tool and a poor selection tool. It tells you which models deserve a trial. It does not tell you which one runs your workload best. For startups priced per task, cheaper near-frontier models widen margins, but only if cost per completed task really falls. Token verbosity and retries can eat a per-token discount. The edge goes to companies with their own evaluation harness built on real customer traces. They can test a new model in days and switch with evidence. The exposure sits with companies whose margin story is a vendor swap justified by a leaderboard. Those companies may find quality slipping on the longest jobs, which are often their highest-value customers. A company with a 'we switched and margins rose' story offers a natural diligence thread. One question worth exploring might be: what did you measure on your own traffic before and after, at what task length, and how did escalations, retries and cost per finished task move? A good answer has numbers from production. A weak one cites the index.

What to watch

Three things would settle this. First, Artificial Analysis' per-test breakdown for Hydrogen against Sonnet, especially on the multi-week business projects and command-line tasks. That tells us where the near-tie comes from. Second, independent long-horizon agent tests on real workloads, run by someone with no model to sell. Those usually lag a launch by weeks. Third, December 1, when Google's bigger models double in price. That will show whether Hydrogen's position is strategy or permanent pricing. Search quality on live queries will also give a public test at enormous scale. If Hydrogen holds up on long agent runs, our view that the gap matters gets weaker.
