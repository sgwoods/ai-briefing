Deep dive: The Third Scaling Law: Paying Models to Think Longer

AI Briefing · 2026-11-03 · follows today's briefing · written and read by AI from public sources

AI models now get better by spending more compute on each answer, not only on training, and that moves cost from the lab's training budget onto every customer's bill. We explain the three scaling laws in plain words, separate what has been shown from what is extrapolated, and look at what it means for inference margins and for startups that price per task.

Why today

Anthropic's IPO filing came out this week, and its headline number is margin. CNBC reports $16.29 billion of revenue in the first half of 2026. Cost of revenue was $2.38 billion. That is an 85% gross margin, and a year earlier the same measure was 12%. List prices per token keep falling. Models close to the frontier now finish many tasks for a fraction of last year's cost. All of those numbers depend on one quantity the headlines rarely name: how much compute a model burns while it answers. For most of the last decade, the big money question in AI was the training run. Now a growing share of capability is bought at answer time, one question at a time. That is the third scaling law. It decides who pays, when they pay, and whether a business that charges per task can keep a margin. So the mechanism is worth getting straight.

Plainly

Start with the three scaling laws. The first is pre-training. You feed a model a huge share of the written world and make it bigger. Within limits, more data and more compute buy a smarter model. The lab pays that bill once, up front. The second is post-training. You take the raw model and coach it with human feedback and reinforcement learning, a method where the model is rewarded for good answers. That teaches it to follow instructions and reason step by step. The lab pays that bill up front too. The third is test-time compute. Here the model itself does not change. You just let it spend more effort on each question. It writes out longer reasoning, tries several answers, checks them and keeps the best one. Here is the analogy. Think of a consulting firm. Pre-training is the consultant's education. Post-training is the firm's in-house training program. The firm carries both as sunk costs. Test-time compute is billable hours. A harder problem gets more hours, and those hours land on the client's invoice every time. That is the shift. The first two laws are capital spending. The third is a cost of goods sold, paid on every job. A worked example makes it concrete. In a 2024 paper called Large Language Monkeys, researchers from Stanford, the University of Oxford and Google DeepMind sampled language models many times on coding tasks, including SWE-bench Lite. With one attempt, it fixed 15.9% of them. With 250 attempts, at least one attempt worked on 56% of them. The best single-attempt result at the time was 43%. The model was the same. They just paid for more tries. But notice the hidden condition. Coding comes with a checker: you can run the tests and see which attempt passed. Without a reliable way to pick the right answer, extra attempts are just more guesses. Keep that in mind. Whether a good checker exists turns out to decide most of this, both for the science and for the business.

What the evidence shows

What has been established is narrower than the hype, but it is real. The result comes from an August 2024 paper by Charlie Snell and colleagues, which appeared at ICLR 2025. They asked a simple question. If a model gets a fixed extra budget at answer time, how much better can it get? The answer depended on how hard the question was. Spending adaptively, more on hard prompts and less on easy ones, was more than 4x more efficient than simply sampling many answers. On problems where a small model already had some success, extra thinking let it beat a model 14x larger at the same compute. Note that condition. Test-time compute amplifies ability the model already has. It did not create ability from nothing. The second solid result is that returns shrink fast as you spend more. The ARC Prize team tested o3 on its ARC-AGI benchmark. It scored 75.7% in the cheaper setting. The high-compute setting reached 87.5% but used about 172x the compute. The ARC Prize team noted that you could pay a human to solve these tasks for roughly $5 each. The third result is about bills. Per-token prices really are collapsing. Epoch AI found that the price of reaching GPT-4's level on PhD-level science questions fell 40x per year. But reasoning models use far more tokens. By that firm's count, o1 generated over 44 million tokens, around eight times as many as GPT-4o. The tokens got cheaper, but each task used more of them. Now for what is claimed but not shown. Labs say their reasoning models improve smoothly as they think longer. Those numbers are self-reported, on benchmarks the labs chose. The bigger extrapolation is that this carries over to open-ended work like strategy, law or medicine. In those fields, no test suite tells you which answer is right. The Monkeys authors found that majority voting and reward models, the usual ways of picking an answer, plateau beyond several hundred samples. As for Anthropic's margin, filing data is stronger than a press release. It still does not tell us how much of that margin comes from heavy reasoning work.

How people read it

The same facts support several honest readings. The AGI-forward reading is that labs now have two dials instead of one. They can turn up thinking time today, without waiting for the next training run. Then they can train the next model on the best reasoning the current one produces, so it gets better without thinking as long. If that loop holds, the steep cost at answer time matters less, because the gains get folded back into training. The AI-realist reading points at the curves. o3's last gain on ARC cost 172x the compute. That is a curve of steeply falling returns, not a breakthrough you can afford everywhere. The clearest wins are in math and code, where answers can be checked. Where nothing checks the answer, more thinking may just produce more confident mistakes. The market reading is about who pays. Toby Ord at Oxford argues that inference scaling pushes AI away from a software business, with cheap extra copies, toward one where each use carries a real cost. As he put it, you do have to pay 10 times as much every time you use it. That favours whoever owns the compute. It also weakens the moat of a single huge training run, which could let more competitors in. The policy reading follows from that. Rules that trigger at a training-compute threshold miss capability bought at answer time. Axios reports that OpenAI plans to push mandatory "speed limits" for powerful AI. Any such rule now has to decide whether it measures training or thinking.

What it means for builders and investors

Our read is this. The third scaling law turns capability into a variable cost, and variable costs are where startups win or lose margin. A company that charges per task now has a cost that swings with how hard each task is. The average may look fine. The hardest few percent of tasks can eat the margin, especially on flat-fee plans where heavy users pay the same as light ones. If the 85% figure holds, lab list prices sit well above the cost of serving. That cuts both ways. Prices have room to keep falling, but the lab sets them, and a startup's cost of goods depends on its choices. We see two places where a seed or Series A company could have an edge. The first is knowing when to think. Send easy work to cheap, fast models and save long reasoning for the hard cases, which is the Snell result turned into a product. The second is owning the checker. A proprietary way to verify answers, whether tests, citations or simulations, is what makes extra compute pay off. The exposed company is a thin wrapper that buys reasoning by the token and resells it at a fixed price. One question worth exploring in diligence: what does a typical task cost, what does one of the hardest cost, and what tells the system its answer is right?

What to watch

Three things would settle much of this. First, Anthropic's quarterly reports as a public company. If gross margin stays near 85% while reasoning-heavy and agent work grows, serving costs are under control. If it slides, the third law is showing up in the cost line. Second, independent cost-per-task leaderboards such as ARC Prize and Artificial Analysis. We want to see whether score per dollar keeps improving, or whether new gains come mostly from spending more. Third, credible evidence that test-time scaling works where answers cannot be checked automatically. A replicated result in law, medicine or open-ended planning would change our view a great deal. Until then, the gains stay concentrated in code and math. The first two signals should arrive within a few quarters. The third has no date.

Steven's take

Increasingly while outputs are often very interesting and valuable people are completely oblivious to what is happening, why the long cycle is better and the expected cost vs value returned, much less the actual cost vs value returned. Software has always had the unique ability to be predictable within well understood error - and building persistent solutions that are testable and cost-predictable where appropriate and when possible should remain the core of how software architectures work. Much can be done to drive value from coding models and other more elaborated problem solving models - but they should not become the on-demand solution for all problems.
