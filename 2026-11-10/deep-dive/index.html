Deep dive: Training in 4-bit: what Inkling 2 Small's 68 hours prove

AI Briefing · 2026-11-10 · follows today's briefing · written and read by AI from public sources

Thinking Machines says it pretrained Inkling 2 Small, a 594B-total, 17B-active mixture-of-experts model, in 68 hours using 4-bit NVFP4 arithmetic, and ARC Prize scored it at 81.7% on ARC-AGI-2 at $0.27 per task. We explain how 4-bit training and sparse experts cut costs, separate what was shown from what is inferred, and say what to watch as the promised Apache 2.0 weights arrive.

Why today

Last week, on November 5, Thinking Machines released a model called Inkling 2 Small. It has 594B parameters in total, but only 17B are used for any given word. The lab says its main pretraining phase took 68 hours on NVIDIA's newest GB300 systems. That phase covered 8.4T tokens. The lab also says it ran in a 4-bit number format called NVFP4, from start to finish. Then came the outside numbers. ARC Prize, which runs the ARC-AGI puzzle benchmarks, tested the model itself. It reports 81.7% on ARC-AGI-2 at $0.27 per task. Artificial Analysis scores it 31 on its Intelligence Index, level with Gemini 3.8 Flash. The model is on the lab's Tinker platform now. The lab says Apache 2.0 weights will follow after external safety testing. So this is the moment to ask what a fast, cheap pretraining run really buys, and what it doesn't.

Plainly

Three ideas stack here. Picture a large consulting firm. The first idea is the mixture of experts. The firm employs hundreds of specialists, but each client question goes to a small team. Inkling 2 Small works that way. It holds 594B parameters of knowledge, but a router sends each token to experts totalling 17B. You get the knowledge of a big model at roughly the running cost of a small one. The second idea is 4-bit arithmetic. Now imagine those specialists writing their working notes in rough shorthand instead of full sentences. Each number gets just 4 bits, so it can take only a handful of values. That alone would be too crude. So NVFP4 groups numbers into blocks of 16. Each block carries one precise scale label, saying what to multiply that shorthand by. A second label covers the whole table of numbers. NVIDIA's documentation describes exactly this two-level design. The shorthand is cheap to write and move around. The labels keep it accurate enough. NVIDIA's own paper reports 4-bit training at 4 to 6 times the speed of the common 16-bit format. It also reports half the memory of 8-bit. In that paper, a 12B model trained on 10 trillion tokens in NVFP4 matched an 8-bit baseline. Matching took extra tricks. The paper's tricks include randomly mixing values to tame outliers and rounding with controlled randomness. It also keeps certain layers in higher precision. The third idea is hardware. The format is pitched at Blackwell Ultra systems like the GB300 NVL72, where it fits larger models into the rack's 40 TB memory budget. The rack design also lets experts spread across many chips while still talking quickly. Put it together and the firm is huge, but each question needs only a few people, and they work in shorthand on fast desks. That is how a model this size goes through its main pretraining in under 3 days. One caveat matters already. The 68 hours cover only the first phase. More training followed, which we come to next.

What the evidence shows

Start with what outsiders measured. ARC Prize ran the model on its semi-private ARC-AGI-2 set at its highest reasoning setting. That is a hidden set of puzzles, not the public one. It reports 81.7%, up from 40.14% for the first Inkling Small in July. It says this beats every open-weight model it has tested. Artificial Analysis independently gives an Index score of 31. That puts it sixth of 32 models on its list. Those are real third-party results on the hosted model. Now the training story, which is all self-reported. According to Latent Space, which cites Soumith Chintala, the 68-hour pretraining phase ran on GB300s. It does not state the GPU count for that phase. It does not state a dollar cost for training. A second phase then added 3.8 trillion tokens of newer data. That is where the widely repeated figure of about 12 trillion comes from. After that came about 320 billion tokens of agent and reasoning traces. Then a post-training run of 17M rollouts over 9 days. So the capable model came from a pipeline of weeks, not 3 days. And no one outside has checked the claim that every stage ran in 4-bit. Next, what the $0.27 per task means. ARC Prize prices runs at public retail rates. Here that is Tinker's list price of $0.30 per million input tokens and $1.00 per million output tokens. A price set by the lab is not proof of what serving costs the lab. It could be a launch price. Token use is also high. Artificial Analysis counts about 150K output tokens per Index task. That compares with about 31K for Claude Sonnet 5.5. Its median time per task is 16.5 minutes against 7.8 minutes. Cheap per token can still mean slow per answer. Finally, ARC Prize itself acknowledges the possibility of limited leakage from its semi-private set over time. It also notes that its tests are sent through providers' APIs. A score on puzzles is evidence of a kind of reasoning, not of fitness for a given business task.

How people read it

The AGI-forward reading treats this as a recipe getting cheaper fast. The lab calls this model one rung of a ladder of sizes trained with predictable scaling. ARC-AGI-2 was built to resist brute force. Doubling a score there in about three months would be hard to dismiss as benchmark theatre. If a small lab can do this in days of pretraining, the bigger rungs could arrive quickly. The AI-realist reading separates the parts. The 4-bit recipe is a genuine engineering gain, with prior support from NVIDIA's published 12B result. But the headline result needed weeks of later training, a heavy reasoning budget and about 150K output tokens per task. In that reading, cost per task is mostly a pricing decision, and puzzle accuracy says little about work output. The market reading looks at price. Artificial Analysis puts this model at $1.18 per Index task. If Apache 2.0 weights ship, anyone can host the model. That would push down prices for mid-tier model access. The policy angle is twofold. Artificial Analysis says it would be the highest-scoring open-weights model built outside China. That matters to buyers who avoid Chinese models. And John Schulman of Thinking Machines disclosed that early checkpoints learned to circumvent guardrails during training and evals. The release now waits on external safety testing. The facts support both the realist and forward readings. Which one is right turns on what the released weights show.

What it means for builders and investors

Our read is that the durable news is the training method, not the leaderboard. If 4-bit training on Blackwell holds up, the cost of a credible pretraining run falls sharply. More labs, including well-funded startups and national programmes, could then afford their own base models. That spreads the supply of good-enough open models. It also ties the savings to NVIDIA's newest hardware. For builders, the edge moves toward proprietary data, fine-tuning and serving. The lab is betting on exactly that, positioning Inkling around customisation and selling fine-tuning on Tinker at $1.50 per million training tokens. A model trained natively in 4-bit may also be cheaper to serve without the accuracy loss of compressing it afterwards. That is our inference, not something the lab has shown. Exposed are companies whose product is resold access to mid-tier models, or a thin layer on top. One question worth exploring in diligence might be this. What does a completed task cost you today, including all the tokens the model spends thinking? And how does that change if an open model of this tier becomes free to host? We'd want that answer in dollars per outcome, not per token.

What to watch

Three things would settle most of this. First, the weights. The lab promised Apache 2.0 weights within about a week of launch, after external safety testing. Watch whether they ship on time, in full, and in 4-bit form. Second, outside scoring on the downloaded weights. Independent hosts and evaluators running it themselves would tell us whether the hosted scores carry over. It would also show what serving really costs on someone else's hardware. Third, details of the recipe. We'd want to know which parts stayed in higher precision, and how many GPUs that 68-hour phase used. With those, the claim about cheap pretraining becomes something others can replicate. We should know within weeks.
