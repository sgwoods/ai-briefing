Deep dive: Is AI Mode Eating Google's Search Money?

AI Briefing · 2026-11-21 · the weekly flagship, two voices · follows today's briefing · written and read by AI from public sources

Google says its AI Mode makes less money per search than the classic results page, in the same quarter that capex pushed Alphabet's free cash flow to negative $9.07B. Bella and Michael look at whether an AI answer can be sold like ten blue links, what that means for funding the AI buildout, and what it leaves for answer-engine startups.

Why today

Bella: On October 28, Alphabet reported its third quarter. Google search revenue grew 12%, to $63.25B by The Register's count. A year earlier, search was growing at 14.5%.

Michael: CNBC printed $63.35B for the same line, so even the headline number wobbles a little. And slower growth on a business that size isn't news on its own, Bella. What makes this quarter different?

Bella: The reason Google gave. Chief business officer Philipp Schindler said "the strong growth in AI Mode queries had an impact because of the lower monetization we were seeing in AI Mode." Google is saying its own AI search earns less.

Michael: That is unusual, I'll grant it. In April 2025 the same executive said of AI Overviews, "We see monetization at the same rate." So the story changed somewhere between those two products.

Bella: On the same day, Google took ads in AI Mode global for the first time and added multi-turn ads in the US. The cash picture was stark too. Capex was $51.36B for the quarter, and free cash flow was negative $9.07B.

Michael: Analysts had expected free cash flow of negative $2.70 billion, per CNBC, but the Google parent came in at negative $9.07 billion. The full-year capex guide stayed at $195B-$205B. CNBC also reported 2027 spending expected above $300B, and the stock fell more than 10%.

Bella: So here's the question for this episode. Can an AI answer be sold the way a page of ten blue links is sold? If the biggest ad machine on the internet earns less per query once it switches, two things follow.

Bella: It decides whether incumbents can pay for this buildout out of their core business. And it decides how much ad money is left over for the startups building answer engines.

Michael: I'll hold you to the fact that this is one quarter. One data point with a confession attached is interesting. It isn't yet a trend.

Plainly

Bella: Start with how classic search makes money. You type a query and Google shows a page. Advertisers pay to appear on that page, and Google earns revenue when someone clicks.

Bella: So revenue is roughly four dials multiplied together. The number of queries, the ads shown per query, the share of people who click, and the price of each click.

Michael: And which of those dials does AI Mode move?

Bella: Potentially all four. Take a query like best running shoes for flat feet. The classic page shows a row of shopping ads, some text ads, then links. Every one of those is a slot somebody bid on.

Bella: In AI Mode you ask a longer question and get a written answer. Google says AI Mode queries average three times longer. The answer might carry one sponsored listing or none, and if it's good, you may never click.

Michael: That's the intuition. But a longer, more specific question is also a sharper signal of what someone wants, and advertisers pay for intent. Couldn't fewer slots each be worth more?

Bella: That's exactly Google's argument. Schindler said "many of these queries are upper funnel queries, allowing us to capture new, high-value commercial intent." Upper funnel means early in the buying journey, researching rather than buying.

Michael: Which is a polite way of saying those queries sit further from a purchase. In most ad markets, early-journey intent sells for less, not more. The promise of value later is carrying a lot of weight in that sentence.

Bella: Here's an analogy. Classic search is a mall directory with paid storefront signs all around it. AI Mode is a concierge who listens to you for a minute, then walks you to one shop.

Bella: The concierge is more useful. But there's only so much signage you can hang on a concierge.

Michael: Then there's the cost side, which the directory never had. A results page is mostly retrieval and ranking. A generated answer means running a model on every query, every time.

Bella: Google says it's pushing that cost down. In its Q2 remarks, Sundar Pichai said Google "reduced the cost of AI Mode responses to its lowest level since launch." He gave no number.

Michael: Lowest since launch gives us the direction, not the level. We don't know whether an AI Mode answer costs a little more than a page of links or a lot more. The margin per query is squeezed at both ends, and we can't see either end.

Michael: Schindler also said "the majority of AI Mode queries are incremental." If that's true, AI Mode isn't eating search. It's adding cheaper queries on top of it.

Bella: And that distinction matters, Michael. Cannibalization means a valuable classic query turns into a cheaper AI query. Incremental means a new query that earns little but still earns something.

Bella: Average revenue per query falls either way. Only one of them shrinks the pie.

Michael: And Google hasn't shown the split. Incremental is self-reported, with no public definition of how it's measured. One last thing a listener would ask: why not just put more ads into the answer?

Bella: Because the answer is the product. A page of links can carry ads beside the results. An answer has to carry them inside the result, and if a sponsored pick seems to bend the answer, people stop trusting it.

What the evidence shows

Bella: Let's separate what's established from what's claimed. Established, from the earnings report: search grew 12% this quarter. CNBC and Search Engine Journal note that was down from 17% in Q2.

Bella: And Q1 was 19%. So that's three straight quarters of slowing growth.

Michael: That trend is real. What else is solid?

Bella: Quarter on quarter, search barely moved. CNBC and Search Engine Journal reported Search at $63.35 billion against $63.27 billion in Q2.

Michael: Flat revenue from one quarter to the next, while TechCrunch says query growth hit its fastest rate in five years. If both hold, revenue per query fell. That's arithmetic, not a claim.

Bella: One caveat. Google didn't disclose the query growth rate, only that it was a five-year high. So we know which way revenue per query moved, but not by how much.

Michael: Now let me press on causation. Yahoo reports management also blamed currency and tough comparisons with World Cup ad spending. So how much of the slowdown is actually AI Mode?

Bella: We don't know. Google didn't split it out. All three explanations are Google's own.

Michael: Which cuts both ways. Companies don't usually volunteer that their flagship product earns less. That makes me take the admission more seriously, not less.

Bella: Now a contested number. Startup Fortune reported that paid-click growth fell from 13% to 8%. Other coverage says Alphabet published no paid-click figures this quarter, and we couldn't confirm it against Alphabet's own materials.

Michael: Then we treat that figure as unconfirmed until it appears in a filing. Revenue per query is the same story. Yahoo and others found no AI Mode figure disclosed, so any number floating around is an estimate.

Bella: Then the claims. First, TechCrunch reports Schindler saying click-through rates on sponsored product listings in AI Mode are already comparable to organic listings.

Michael: Comparable to organic listings, not to classic search ads. That's a different baseline. It's also one ad format, picked by Google, with no number attached.

Bella: Second, the history. In 2025, Schindler and Pichai both said ads around AI Overviews earned at about the same rate as a regular results page.

Michael: So either AI Mode is a different product economically, or the earlier claim described a short, flattering window. In Q2, Google said it had folded AI Overviews and AI Mode into "one seamless Search experience." That makes the old same-rate claim hard to apply now.

Bella: Third, the forward claim: global ads close the gap over time. That's untested. The rollout was announced the same day as earnings, so there isn't a single quarter of data yet.

Michael: The formats tell you something, though. Conversational discovery ads, sponsored recommendations inside answers, multi-turn ads. Google is inventing new ad space because the old ad space is shrinking.

Bella: Last, the cash. Capex and free cash flow are reported numbers, not estimates. Net income fell to $26.17B from $34.98B a year earlier, per CNBC, which didn't give the cause.

Michael: Here I'll push the other way, Bella. Free cash flow went negative because of capex, not because search collapsed. And Cloud grew 103% to $30.7B, so a lot of that capex has paying Cloud customers, not AI Mode users.

Bella: That's fair, and it matters. The capex doesn't only serve search. But search is still just under half of Alphabet's revenue, per The Register, and it's the cash engine the buildout has leaned on.

How people read it

Bella: So how do serious people read the same facts? Start with the AGI-forward reading. Answers are the better product, users are choosing them, and query growth proves it. Money follows attention, and ads will follow intent.

Michael: The strongest version of that: Pichai noted that Google's Gemini app has won a billion users faster than any other product in the company's history. Google also runs the best ad auction on earth. If anyone can price an answer, it's Google, and today's dip is the cost of moving first.

Bella: Then the AI-realist reading. An answer replaces the click, and the click was the business. Fewer slots, cheaper early-journey intent, a higher cost to serve. On that reading the margin per query is thinner for good, not for a phase.

Michael: Realists would point to how the language moved. Same rate for AI Overviews in 2025, then lower monetization for AI Mode now. Executives don't usually change wording like that unless the numbers make them.

Bella: Then the market reading, which is more about cash timing than technology. The stock fell more than 10% in a quarter when Cloud more than doubled. Investors seem to be pricing a capex bill that arrives before the AI revenue does.

Michael: There's a twist inside that reading. Cloud's 103% growth suggests the buildout has customers paying rent on it. If that holds, search doesn't have to carry the whole bill.

Bella: Partly. But some of those customers are AI labs that are hungry for cash themselves. This week Google was one of seven companies buying into Anthropic's $26B private placement, alongside its IPO.

Bella: That's a cloud provider putting equity into a lab that rents cloud compute.

Michael: That's a fair hit on circular money. But an equity stake in a customer doesn't make the revenue fake. We'd need Cloud's customer concentration before saying how much it matters.

Bella: So where do these readings really part ways? On one variable: whether revenue per AI answer climbs back toward revenue per classic query. The AGI-forward reading says yes, given time. The realist reading says it settles lower.

Michael: And honestly, one quarter can't tell those two apart. Both predict exactly what we saw. That's why I keep resisting a verdict.

What it means for builders and investors

Bella: Here's our read, held provisionally. We think this quarter weakens the idea that incumbents can comfortably fund the AI buildout out of search. Google's own AI product is cited as slowing its own growth, while capex runs ahead of cash.

Michael: I'd put it more softly. Google still grew search 12% and earned $26.17B in net income this quarter. This is a margin story, not a solvency story.

Bella: Agreed. Now the startups. If the best ad machine on the internet earns less per AI answer, an ad-funded answer engine starts out worse. It has a thinner auction, fewer advertisers, and pays someone else's price for inference.

Michael: Unless it picks queries where the answer sits right next to a transaction. Commerce, travel, a professional task. There you can charge per outcome or per seat instead of per ad shown.

Bella: That's where we land too. The room for ad-funded general search looks smaller than it did a year ago. The room for answers tied to a purchase, a subscription or a workflow looks untouched by this quarter.

Michael: There's a second group to think about: companies that grow on Google traffic. If answers absorb the click, organic search becomes a less dependable way to acquire customers.

Bella: One question worth exploring in diligence: what does the company earn per answer, what does each answer cost to serve, and how do both move as conversations get longer? Queries three times longer are good for engagement and can be bad for gross margin.

Michael: And for a company that depends on search for growth, we'd want to see acquisition broken out by channel over several quarters. A blended customer acquisition cost hides exactly this shift.

Bella: Where we still disagree. I think the gap is structural. An answer has less room for ads than a page of links, and I don't expect that to fully close.

Michael: I think it's too early to call. This quarter carries currency and World Cup noise, and the global ads launched on earnings day. I'd want two more quarters of search revenue before calling anything structural.

What to watch

Bella: Three things would settle this. First, Alphabet's Q4 call. Anat Ashkenazi, the chief financial officer, said Google would "provide further details on our Q4 call" about 2027 capex.

Michael: On that call, watch whether search picks up again once global AI Mode ads have a full quarter behind them. If search revenue stays flat from one quarter to the next, the over-time claim gets much harder to defend.

Bella: Second, disclosure. Does Google ever publish AI Mode revenue per query, or paid clicks and cost per click, in its filings? Until it does, every revenue-per-answer number out there is a guess.

Michael: Third, the cash. If 2027 capex goes past $300B, as CNBC reported is expected, and free cash flow stays negative, the funding question gets louder for everyone downstream.

Bella: What would change our view? Two quarters where search growth holds up while AI Mode's share of queries rises. That would show answers can be sold like links.

Michael: And what would change mine is another flat quarter with clean comparisons and stable currency. At that point I'd stop blaming the World Cup.
