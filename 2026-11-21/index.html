1. Claude-assisted and human-only Riemann Hypothesis proofs claimed the same week

TechCrunch AI · Latent Space · Simon Willison · 1 report + 1 hands-on test + 1 commentary · source

  • What happened: Anthropic's Levent Alpöge claims a Claude-assisted, Lean-compilable proof of the Riemann Hypothesis (1.3GB/250k pages). MIT's Andrew Sutherland has independently confirmed that it compiles. Stanford's Ravi Vakil announces Alessandro Masullo's human-only Generalized Riemann Hypothesis proof (940-page draft), which Brian Conrad has reviewed but which is not yet verified as RH. (TechCrunch AI, Latent Space, Simon Willison)
  • Evidence check: Reported. Alpöge's Lean code compiles independently confirmed; both proofs' validity remains unverified beyond compilation and review stages.
  • Our read: We think a Lean proof that compiles under an outside check is the most checkable form this claim could take. What remains is whether the formal statement really is RH, an audit measured in weeks, not years. (House view: A year thick with checkable science)
  • The case for: MIT's Andrew Sutherland independently confirmed the Lean proof compiles; a machine-checked proof doesn't depend on referees reading 250k pages, so checking reduces to auditing statement and axioms.
  • The case against: Compilation shows Lean accepted the code, not that it proves RH; the claim comes from Anthropic staff, and validity beyond compilation remains unverified.
  • The deciding question: Does an independent audit confirm the Lean theorem statement is RH, with no added axioms?
  • Our lean: Under our checkable-science view we lean for: compiled Lean is the strongest evidence form, and the remaining audit is small and checkable.
  • For early-stage: A reference case for formal-verification tooling: startups selling auditable AI reasoning, proof checking or statement auditing now have a 1.3GB example of why buyers might need it.

2. OpenAI says coding-agent success doubled; Ramez Naam says horizons still lag forecasts

Chandrakumar R Pillai · Ramez Naam · primary source + 1 commentary · source

  • What happened: OpenAI's Nov. 18 internal analysis shows the zero-intervention success rate for tasks sized at 2–4 human hours rose from 23% (March) to 46% (October). It attributes part of the rise to changed delegation choices (Chandrakumar R Pillai). Ramez Naam cites OpenAI's 80%-success internal horizon as growing slower than METR-based forecasts, noting real-world task horizons lag predictions (Ramez Naam).
  • Evidence check: Self-reported. OpenAI's self-reported success rate increase is established but potentially inflated by delegation shifts; Naam's claim relies on labs' unverified data and short timeframe.
  • Our read: We read the doubling as a lab's number about its own product, partly explained by changes in which tasks users delegated. We would want success on a fixed task set before pricing an agent business on it. (House view: Weigh the claim by the claimant)
  • For early-stage: For agent startups priced per task, OpenAI's 23% to 46% doesn't transfer; the number that moves margins is their own zero-intervention rate on customer-chosen tasks.
  • The AGI-forward view: A doubling in seven months on multi-hour tasks fits compounding capability; changed delegation may itself reflect users trusting agents with more, which is the adoption signal.
