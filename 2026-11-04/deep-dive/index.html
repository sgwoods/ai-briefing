Deep dive: Robot Foundation Models: Does Scale Work for Atoms?

AI Briefing · 2026-11-04 · follows today's briefing · written and read by AI from public sources

Eka Robotics raised a $150M Series A at a $1.45B valuation on a self-reported pick rate, and rivals claim scaling laws for robots. We explain how robot models learn from simulation, teleoperation and video, what has actually been shown, and which metrics tell a working system from a demo.

Why today

Eka Robotics, a Cambridge, Massachusetts startup founded in 2024, has raised a $150M Series A at a $1.45B post-money valuation. Andreessen Horowitz and F-Prime led the round. Eka is led by MIT professor Pulkit Agrawal and Tuomas Haarnoja, who used to be at Google DeepMind. Its product is a set of robotic systems driven by what it calls a Vision-Force-Action model. Eka says it does about 1,500 picks per hour. It puts a human worker at around 600. No customer has been named and no production deployment has been described. So a Series A is being priced on numbers the company reports about itself. Eka is not alone. Physical Intelligence is reportedly valued at $11 billion, and Generalist at roughly twice Eka's $1.45 billion. The story is that the recipe that worked for text will work for robots. More data, bigger models, predictable gains. That recipe deserves a hard look now, before more capital is committed on the strength of it.

Plainly

Start with why scale worked for text. Language models learned from a library people had already written. Trillions of words were sitting on the internet, free to collect. Make the model bigger, feed it more text, and its errors fell along a smooth, predictable curve. Investors could plan around that curve. Robots don't have that library. Each useful example is a record of a physical action: what the camera saw, what the arm did, what the fingers felt, and whether it worked. Someone has to produce every one of those records. There are three ways to do it. The first is teleoperation, where a person remote-controls a robot and every movement is logged. That gives high-quality data, but it is slow and you pay for every hour. The second is simulation, where a physics engine runs millions of virtual attempts. That is cheap and fast, but the virtual world is never quite the real one. Friction, soft materials and glare all come out slightly wrong. The third is human video, which exists in huge amounts but shows only what happened, not how it felt. Here is the analogy to keep in mind. Picture a teenager who has watched ten thousand hours of driving videos. They know what parallel parking looks like. Put them in a real car and they still clip the curb, because the feel of the pedal and the wheel was never in the footage. Robot manipulation is mostly that feel. It is how hard to squeeze, when something slips, when a screw has caught its thread. That is why Eka puts force at the centre of its pitch. It says it builds what it calls an intuition of physics through reinforcement learning at scale. In plain words, the robot practises by trial and error in simulation, gets rewarded when it succeeds, and adjusts. In Eka's words, robots should master physics, not mimic human data. The open question is whether that practice carries over to a real factory floor.

What the evidence shows

Some of this is genuinely established. In March, a team published MolmoBot, a model trained only on simulated data. They released MolmoBot-Data, a dataset of 1.8 million expert trajectories for articulated object manipulation and pick-and-place tasks. With no real-world training at all, it reached 79.2% success in real tabletop tests across four settings. The comparison model they tested scored 39.2%. That is a real result, in a paper, against a named baseline. A second paper, DexSim2Real, reports a 78.2% average real-world success rate across six challenging manipulation tasks. Its testing was blinded. So simulation can now take a robot from nothing to working most of the time on bounded tasks. Notice what most of the time means, though. On scaling, the evidence is thinner. A 2024 paper by Sebastian Sartor and Neil Thompson, titled Neural Scaling Laws in Robotics, examined how scaling laws apply to robotics. It found robot performance improving with data, model size and compute, along power-law curves. That is suggestive. But a meta-analysis across different labs, robots and tasks is a weaker kind of evidence than one controlled experiment. The strongest scaling claim comes from Generalist, about its own model, GEN-0. It says it trained on over 270,000 hours of real manipulation data, growing by 10,000 hours a week. It reports a power-law link between how much it pretrains and how well the model does afterwards. It says models below about 7B parameters struggle to absorb the data. Those are interesting findings. They are also self-reported, measured mostly as prediction error, and not independently replicated. Then there is Eka. The 1,500 picks per hour figure comes from the company. We found no published method, no description of the objects or the test conditions, and no outside audit. One analysis of the round put the next proof point plainly. It is a named customer running the robots on a production line for months, not minutes.

How people read it

The facts allow more than one honest reading. The AGI-forward reading goes like this. Text models also looked brittle before scale. Now there are smooth curves from Generalist and in the meta-analysis. Simulation results jumped within a year, and data collection is speeding up. Andreessen Horowitz, which led Eka's round, is plainly in this camp. Its Raghu Raghuram called Eka's model the first approach he had seen that truly solves manipulation with the generality and throughput deployments need. The AI-realist reading starts from the same facts and lands somewhere else. Rodney Brooks, who co-founded iRobot, is particularly skeptical of companies like Tesla and Figure trying to teach robots dexterity by showing them videos of humans doing tasks. In his words, we don't have such a tradition for touch data. Speech and vision had decades of collected data behind them. Touch has very little. A realist also notes that falling prediction error is not the same as fewer dropped parts. And the last few percent of reliability is usually where robotics projects die. The market reading is narrower. Buyers don't pay for generality. They pay for uptime on a specific line. Where the readings agree is simple. Nobody has yet shown, in public, a general manipulation model holding up over months in a customer's plant.

What it means for builders and investors

Our read is that simulation-trained manipulation is real and improving fast. But the scaling story for robots is still a hypothesis, backed mostly by companies measuring themselves. That changes what can be built now. Bounded jobs look within reach: picking, kitting, simple assembly, with fixed objects and forgiving tolerances. Open-ended dexterity in messy settings does not yet. Serving costs are different from software. Each deployment brings hardware, integration work, sensors that wear out, and someone to step in when the robot fails. Margins depend on how often that person is needed, not only on the model. For a seed or Series A company, the edge may not be owning the biggest model. It may be owning a workflow, the data that comes back from real deployments, and the customer relationship. The exposure is the reverse case: a thin application layer built on someone else's robot model, with no data of its own. One question worth exploring in diligence might be the intervention rate. How many times per hour, or per shift, does a person have to step in, on a real customer line, measured over weeks? We'd also want the success rate on objects the system has never seen, and the method behind any picks-per-hour figure.

What to watch

Three things would settle a lot of this. First, a named customer publishing uptime and intervention data after months of production, from Eka or any of its peers. Its own analysts say that is the next proof point, so it could come within a year. Second, independent replication of a robot scaling curve. That would mean a lab with no commercial stake showing that more data improves real task success, not just prediction error. Third, shared benchmarks for dexterity and force, run under blinded conditions, the way DexSim2Real was tested. If those arrive and the curves hold, the AGI-forward reading gains ground. If deployments stall at the last few percent of reliability, the realists were right.

Steven's take

Determining what information is explictly embedded in models as part of the data ingestion process and what is implicitly embedded is extremely challenging and in most real-world atom-moving solutions the gap is critical and thus we need to have a very real "show me" mentality for what is a fantastic demo and what is a realistic in-production approach and this is very problem specific.
