Deep dive: Taiwan Risk: What Anthropic's S-1 Says About Compute

AI Briefing · 2026-11-18 · follows today's briefing · written and read by AI from public sources

Anthropic's IPO filing says its business depends on advanced chips made mostly in Taiwan, mostly by TSMC, and that building capacity elsewhere is still at an early stage. We look at how concentrated frontier compute really is, what Arizona does and doesn't fix by 2028, and what this means for startups whose gross margin sits on someone else's API.

Why today

Anthropic filed its S-1, the registration statement a company files before listing shares, on October 26. Most of the coverage since has been about revenue growth and valuation. One passage in the risk factors deserves more attention. In the filing's words, "The majority of the world's most advanced chip manufacturing capacity is concentrated in Taiwan, primarily at Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company." The filing goes on to name earthquakes, typhoons and potential disruption from geopolitical tensions between Taiwan and China as risks. It warns that the effects of a disruption could last for years. Chipmakers have disclosed this kind of risk for a long time. This is the first time a frontier model lab has had to state it in a document it can be sued over if the document misleads. That changes the question. Model access is now a geopolitical supply risk as well as a price risk.

Plainly

Start with what a frontier lab buys. It rents or owns accelerators: Nvidia GPUs, Google TPUs and Amazon Trainium chips. According to Tom's Hardware, Anthropic told IPO investors it runs its models on all of these and on AMD GPUs too. CNBC reports that Anthropic treats them as a single fungible pool. That sounds like diversification. In one sense it is. Anthropic is not locked into one chip designer, one software stack or one cloud. But look one layer down. Nvidia's Blackwell is built on a TSMC process. Google's Ironwood TPU is reported to use TSMC's 3 nanometre process. So is Amazon's Trainium3. Then there is packaging, the step that joins the compute die to its stacks of high-bandwidth memory. For Nvidia's top parts, The Register reports that all of the CoWoS packaging, TSMC's method for this, currently happens in Taiwan. Here is an analogy. Imagine a company that spread its freight across four airlines to avoid depending on any one of them. Then it finds that all four fly out of the same airport. Spreading across carriers protects you from one airline's strike. It does nothing about fog at the airport. Anthropic's fungible pool is four carriers and one airport. Now walk through what a disruption would do. The chips already installed in data centres keep running. A blockade doesn't switch off a GPU in Virginia. What stops is new supply. Training the next frontier model needs a new cluster, usually bigger than the last one, so training would feel it first. Serving would feel it next, because demand keeps growing while capacity stays fixed. Hardware also wears out and goes obsolete. A lab facing that would have to ration. It would weigh new training runs against its own products and against its largest API customers. Smaller customers would probably be served last. Startups that buy from these labs should keep that ordering in mind.

What the evidence shows

Some of this is well established. Anthropic's risk factor language is primary text, quoted the same way by several outlets. It also says that TSMC "has, at times, been unable to fully satisfy market demand" and that Anthropic has "not always been able to obtain sufficient quantities of chips on a timely basis." So the filing records actual past shortfalls, not just a hypothetical disruption. That figure is five years old, and the industry body that published it lobbies for US chip subsidies. Nothing we found suggests the picture has fundamentally changed for the most advanced nodes. Nvidia's own annual reports have flagged Taiwan for years. They say it relies heavily on TSMC and that natural disasters in Taiwan could limit wafer supply. The Arizona timeline is reported, not guaranteed. TSMC's first Arizona fab produced a Blackwell wafer in October 2025, but those wafers still go back to Taiwan for packaging. Reports put the second fab's 3 nanometre volume production in late 2027. They put 2 nanometre in Arizona at 2029 or later. Amkor's Arizona packaging plant is expected to start production in early 2028. The advanced packaging plant near TSMC's Arizona fab isn't TSMC's own but Amkor's, and it is still only planned. Anthropic's filing itself says these efforts outside Taiwan are in early stages. It also says they may not reduce the risk within the foreseeable future. Some things are claimed but not shown. The filing gives no breakdown of how much of Anthropic's compute depends on Taiwan wafers or Taiwan packaging, and no estimate of how long its installed fleet could hold out. That figure is self-reported and unaudited by outside analysts so far. The filing itself cautions that margins will fluctuate over time, particularly in periods when substantial incremental compute is delivered.

How people read it

The same facts read differently depending on what you expect from AI. The AGI-forward reading says compute is the binding constraint on progress. On that view, a Taiwan disruption would freeze the frontier at whatever had already been trained. Whoever held the most installed capacity would hold the lead for years, which makes onshoring urgent. The AI-realist reading starts with lawyers. Risk factors are written to cover every bad outcome, and Nvidia has carried similar language for years without a disruption. On this view, the likely case is chronic tightness and rising prices, not a blockade. Installed capacity and today's models would keep serving for a long time. The market reading looks at who would benefit. If new supply stopped, anyone already holding installed GPUs would gain pricing power. Nvidia is also in talks, according to The Information, to provide Nscale with billions of dollars in credit guarantees for debt to develop a 460MW data center that Anthropic plans to use. Reportedly the guarantee covers power and the building, not the chips. Even with that financing, every chip in that hall still comes from the same supply chain. The policy reading is the most contested one. A Wall Street Journal column by Joseph Sternberg carries the headline Anthropic's IPO Will Be a National Security Win for Taiwan. We could not read the full argument. The headline idea seems to be that dependence binds US capital to Taiwan's security. The opposite risk is just as plausible: it makes the island an even more valuable pressure point.

What it means for builders and investors

Our read is that this disclosure is about the whole sector, not Anthropic in particular. Every frontier lab, and every hyperscaler designing its own chip, depends on the same fabs and the same packaging lines. For a startup built on a model API, the usual worry is price: will tokens get cheaper or dearer? The S-1 adds a second worry, which is allocation. If supply tightens, the lab chooses who gets served. A startup's gross margin and its rate limits then depend on a decision it has no part in. This doesn't make API-dependent startups bad investments. Supply shocks might even help companies whose products can switch between providers or run smaller open-weight models on hardware already installed. Those models still run on TSMC silicon. Their advantage is that they need less of it and can run on older parts. One question worth exploring in diligence: what happens to this company if its main model provider halves its capacity for a year? We'd want to know whether the founders have tested switching providers. We'd also want to know what gross margin looks like on a fallback model, and whether their contract says anything about allocation priority. We hold this view provisionally. A credible schedule for packaging outside Taiwan would weaken it a lot.

What to watch

Three things would settle this. First, the amended S-1s. If SEC comments or underwriter diligence produce a sharper risk factor that puts numbers on how much compute depends on Taiwan, that tells us more than the current language does. Second, the Arizona milestones. Reports point to late 2027 for 3 nanometre volume there and early 2028 for Amkor's packaging plant. If either slips, the window of full concentration gets longer. Third, other labs. If another frontier lab files to go public, compare its risk factors with Anthropic's. If the language matches, this is a sector-wide fact, not one company's lawyers being cautious.
