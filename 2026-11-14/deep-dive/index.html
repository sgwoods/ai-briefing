Deep dive: Nvidia Without Jensen Huang: What a Founder Exit Puts at Risk

AI Briefing · 2026-11-14 · the weekly flagship, two voices · follows today's briefing · written and read by AI from public sources

Jensen Huang has resigned from Nvidia while a board probe runs and the FBI looks into a matter of unstated scope. We separate the confirmed facts from the claims, then follow the parts of the AI economy that run through Nvidia's decisions: chip allocation, supply, vendor financing, country-level deals and its investments in other companies.

Why today

Bella: On November 12, Nvidia said Jensen Huang had resigned as president, chief executive and board member, effective immediately. CFO Colette Kress is interim CEO and keeps the finance job. Harvey Jones, a former Synopsys chief executive, becomes the board's first-ever chair.

Michael: Let's be careful about what that release actually says. It says nothing about any investigation. It says the board expects operations, customer commitments and the product roadmap "to continue without interruption." That's the company describing itself.

Bella: Right, so the context comes from elsewhere. On November 8 the board announced an independent investigation, overseen by independent directors with outside counsel. It followed reports that Huang had been the target of a foreign intelligence effort. Two days later, President Trump said the FBI was looking into it.

Michael: Said, publicly. Nobody has said what the FBI is examining, or whether Huang faces any allegation. And the Wall Street Journal report that the board was about to fire him reached most people through commentary. Nvidia never confirmed a firing.

Bella: Fair. So the confirmed list is short: a resignation, interim leadership, a board probe and an FBI inquiry of unstated scope. Senate Banking chair Tim Scott has also asked Huang and Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick to testify in December. Lutnick has agreed.

Michael: Then why spend a whole episode on it? Chips don't stop shipping because a CEO leaves.

Bella: Because Nvidia isn't only a chip vendor. It decides who gets scarce supply, signs deals with countries, backs other companies' financing and invests in startups. The Robot Report puts the stock down about 18.5% across the week. For builders, the question is whether to wait this out or price it into roadmaps and runway now.

Michael: Okay. Then I want to know which of those depended on one man, and which on the company.

Plainly

Bella: Start with how Nvidia was run. SemiAnalysis, a chip research firm that sells supply chain data to the industry, says Huang had roughly 60 direct reports. Huang has described that structure himself, and says he skips one-on-ones.

Michael: The 60 figure comes from his own public statements, so I'll take it. What's the claim built on top of it?

Bella: That the big decisions ran through him. In SemiAnalysis's words, the company "was never built for him not to be there." Allocation is the clearest case. When demand outruns supply, someone chooses whether a hyperscaler, a neocloud or a national buyer gets the next batch.

Michael: That's plausible coming from a firm with good sources. It's also impossible to check from outside. We never see an allocation decision. We only see who ends up holding GPUs.

Bella: Agreed, so here's the part with numbers. Picture a parent co-signing a mortgage. The bank gives the kid a good rate because the parent stands behind the loan. SemiAnalysis says Nvidia plays that parent for neoclouds.

Michael: Walk me through how that actually works.

Bella: Nvidia sells the GPUs at full margin on day one. Then, per SemiAnalysis, it guarantees a floor under the buyer's revenue, guarantees the buyer's landlord, or signs the lease itself. The upshot, they write, is that "operators without an investment-grade rating can borrow at investment-grade pricing."

Michael: So part of a neocloud's cost of capital is really Nvidia's balance sheet. How big is this?

Bella: SemiAnalysis says Nvidia disclosed $530B of gross off-balance-sheet guarantees as of its fiscal second quarter. That's up from $184B the quarter before. Within that, they cite $36B of take-or-pay floors, meaning guaranteed minimum payments, under neocloud capacity.

Michael: I'd want to see the filing before repeating $530B as fact. That's SemiAnalysis reading Nvidia's disclosures, and the word gross is doing a lot of work. Gross exposure isn't expected loss.

Bella: Right, and we couldn't confirm that line ourselves. But the co-signer logic doesn't depend on the exact number. A lender pricing a neocloud loan is pricing the co-signer. When the co-signer's leadership changes overnight, the lender rereads the terms.

Michael: Here's my push. The co-signer is Nvidia the corporation, not Jensen the person. Contracts don't resign. A guarantee signed in May still holds in November.

Bella: For signed contracts, yes. The risk sits in the next ones. SemiAnalysis calls the market's faith in him "the Jensen put." An interim CEO with a finance background, running a company under a board probe, may write new backstops more slowly.

Michael: Or say no more often. Which a CFO might well be right to do.

Bella: Possibly. Now the supply side. SemiAnalysis says Nvidia's supply and capacity commitments jumped from $119B to $279B in one quarter, mostly for memory. Their claim is that Huang personally locked up memory in Korea and chip manufacturing in Taiwan.

Michael: For a startup, that one cuts both ways. If Nvidia's grip on memory loosens, rivals like AMD might ship more. That's good news for anyone who wants a second supplier.

Bella: It is. A quieter channel is country-level deals. The Robot Report cites Reuters and Morningstar saying his departure could slow AI deals and draw more government scrutiny of trade deals for sovereign AI.

Michael: Could slow is analysts guessing. Still, I'll grant that those deals get signed at the top, so an empty seat at the top matters more there.

Bella: Last comes investment. In September Nvidia announced a $12.9 billion deal for Hugging Face. Gary Marcus, a longtime critic of AI hype, also points to billions he says Nvidia promised Anthropic around its IPO.

Michael: Marcus poses that as a question, not a reported fact. He asked whether an acting CEO "still goes through with the billions they promised Anthropic in the IPO." It's a fair question that nobody can answer yet.

Bella: So, plainly: several channels run through Nvidia's decisions. Who gets chips, whose financing gets backed, which countries get deals and which companies get Nvidia's money. The useful question is which of those touches your company.

Michael: And mine is which of them truly depended on the man. The plumbing still exists without him.

What the evidence shows

Bella: Let's sort it out. Nvidia's own release establishes the resignation, Kress as interim CEO and CFO, and Jones as chair. Earnings are still scheduled for November 17.

Michael: Agreed, that's solid. What about the probe?

Bella: Also established. The board announced it on November 8. According to eeNews Europe, it said it had "no reason to believe that US national security or confidential Nvidia information had been compromised."

Michael: And four days later the CEO is gone. Either the board learned something new, or that November 8 statement was optimistic. From the outside, we can't tell which.

Bella: That's fair. In his own November 8 statement, quoted by The Robot Report, Huang wrote "you deserve better from me" and pledged to cooperate. The Robot Report says he admitted to affairs, one with an executive at Megaspeed, a data center firm.

Michael: That's where I'd slow down. The suspected spy ties are a claim from the press and from intelligence sources. The FBI's involvement rests on the president's public remarks. Nobody has said what the bureau is actually examining.

Bella: Correct. Wire reports also have Trump saying he spoke to Huang and believes Huang didn't know about the alleged ties. That's a president describing a phone call. It isn't an investigative finding.

Michael: Now the claims I'd mark as unshown. SemiAnalysis says the resignation is "directly tied to the vast smuggling of GPUs." They say their own model shows more than 1.5GW of restricted Nvidia chips ended up in China.

Bella: That's a proprietary model from a firm that benefits from being the one that called it. It may be right. But it estimates where chips went. It isn't evidence of what the board knew or why it acted.

Michael: Ed Zitron goes further. In posts and TV appearances, he says smuggling may account for as much as 20% of Nvidia's fiscal 2026 revenue, about $43bn. That's commentary from someone whose whole beat is that AI financing is unsound.

Bella: And Nvidia hasn't said anything like it. If a fifth of revenue were at risk, we'd expect to see it in the November 17 results or the risk disclosures. That gets tested within days.

Michael: Next, the Journal's firing report. Marcus wrote that the Journal reported Nvidia was "about to fire him." We couldn't find the Journal article itself, so we're relying on his summary.

Bella: And events overtook it. Pushed or not, he's out. What matters more is Marcus's next step: that Huang is "the ringmaster of all the insane circular financing," and "all that might collapse."

Michael: For me that's the weakest link in the chain. Circular financing means Nvidia backs customers who then buy Nvidia chips. That structure is real. But might collapse is a prediction, and not a new one.

Bella: Then let's test it against this week's credit news. Bloomberg's sources say CoreWeave plans to raise at least $4B through an investment-grade loan. CoreWeave says it may yield 7%-7.5%, about half what its junk bonds pay.

Michael: If it closes on those terms, lenders aren't panicking about the neocloud model. That answer comes from the market, not from a pundit.

Bella: But look at how neoclouds are paying for growth. TechCrunch reports CoreWeave is asking customers to pay up front and moving to shorter contracts that could mean higher prices. Nebius plans $40 billion of spending, funded partly by customer prepayments.

Michael: So the risk doesn't vanish. It shifts onto the customer, and often that customer's runway. I'll give the bears that point.

Bella: One more claim. SemiAnalysis argues a non-founder CEO won't make long bets like CUDA, which lost money for years. That's an argument from history, not evidence.

Michael: And it skips past the professionally managed giants that have made very large AI bets. I'd hold that one loosely.

How people read it

Bella: Same facts, different readings. The AGI-forward reading says demand for compute is structural. TechCrunch reports OpenAI's quarterly revenue crossed $10 billion, up from $6.7 billion. If demand keeps growing like that, buyers keep buying GPUs whoever runs Nvidia.

Michael: That's a reported figure, not an audited one, and revenue isn't margin. But I'll grant that demand isn't what changed this week.

Bella: On that reading, the moat is the software stack and the installed base, not the man. One headline from The Neuron sums it up: "Nvidia Will Probably Be Fine—Eventually."

Michael: The AI-realist reading lands somewhere else. It says the hardware moat survives, but the financing scaffolding was personal. As SemiAnalysis puts it, neoclouds raised money "because Jensen backed them." Take away the backer and capital gets dearer at the margin.

Bella: On financing, that's the reading I find most convincing. It doesn't need a crash. It only needs new backstops to get smaller or slower.

Michael: I'd call that plausible but unproven. We haven't seen a single deal pulled.

Bella: The market reading is the share price. The Robot Report says it fell about 18.5% across the week, for a company valued at about $4 trillion.

Michael: Prices tell you uncertainty rose. They don't tell you which part the market is afraid of. It could be governance, China revenue, or the founder premium walking out the door.

Bella: Then there's the policy reading, which may move fastest. SemiAnalysis expects China restrictions to "ramp now much faster" with Huang gone. It points to RASA, a bill it says has already passed the House.

Michael: SemiAnalysis says openly that it dislikes how RASA is drafted and prefers other rules, so it has a view to sell. Still, a Senate Banking hearing in December is a real date on the calendar.

Bella: There's also an open-source angle. SemiAnalysis calls it "an open secret" that Nvidia helps Chinese labs optimize their models. TechCrunch asks whether Nvidia's support for open models, and the Hugging Face deal, will last. Hugging Face's CEO has vowed to keep fighting for open source AI.

Michael: For startups building on Chinese open-weight models, that's real exposure. But that policy risk existed before this week. Huang's exit may only speed it up.

Bella: That's where the readings really split. Not on whether Nvidia survives, but on speed: how fast financing tightens, and how fast export rules move.

What it means for builders and investors

Bella: Here's our read. We think this is a governance shock, not yet a supply shock. Chips keep shipping under an interim CEO, and signed contracts stand. But companies whose roadmaps assume one vendor's pricing and allocation look more exposed this week than last.

Michael: I'll sign the first half. The second half needs a mechanism, and I'd keep it narrow. Personal discretion mattered most in three places: new backstops, new allocation promises and new strategic investments.

Bella: Agreed. For builders the exposure is mostly indirect. If you rent GPUs from a neocloud, your price may depend on that neocloud's financing. If that financing leaned on an Nvidia floor, your terms could change at renewal.

Michael: And prepayments already push that risk onto you. CoreWeave asking for cash up front is a runway question, not a GPU question.

Bella: So one question worth exploring in diligence might be this. If a company's compute provider raised prices or demanded prepayment within a year, how much runway would that cost? And could the workload move?

Michael: I'd add a second. Does the inference stack run on a non-Nvidia accelerator today, or is the second supplier only on a slide? A backup that's never been tested isn't really a backup.

Bella: For infrastructure investors, we'd want to know which neocloud loans lean on Nvidia guarantees, and what conditions sit inside them. For startups that took Nvidia money, we'd want to know whether future installments carry conditions.

Michael: Here's where we still disagree, Bella. You think allocation power was concentrated in one person. I think it was concentrated in a scarce product. Scarcity allocates itself, whoever signs the memo.

Bella: I'd push back a little. Scarcity sets the price. Someone still picks between a hyperscaler and a neocloud on the backstop program. That choice now sits with a CFO under a board probe.

Michael: And a more cautious chooser might be healthier for everyone. Fewer clusters financed by the vendor means less circularity.

Bella: Possibly. What would change our mind? If new Nvidia financing deals keep closing at the old pace and neocloud credit stays cheap, then this was a governance event and nothing more.

What to watch

Bella: Three things should settle it. First, November 17, when Nvidia reports quarterly results. Watch the guarantee and supply commitment lines, any disclosure about the investigation, and how China revenue is described.

Michael: And listen for whether the interim team reaffirms existing deals, including Hugging Face and anything tied to Anthropic. Silence on those would tell us something.

Bella: Second, credit. Bloomberg's sources say CoreWeave's investment-grade loan could come as soon as next week. If it prices near 7%-7.5%, lenders see no contagion. If it widens or stalls, the financing risk is real.

Michael: Watch neocloud contract terms too. More demands for prepayment would show the risk moving onto customers, even if the loans close.

Bella: Third, Washington. Senate Banking hearings are set for December, and Lutnick has agreed to appear. Whether Huang testifies, and whether RASA-style restrictions move forward, will shape export rules for every chip vendor.

Michael: I'd add one more: who gets the permanent job. A long-serving engineer signals continuity. An outsider signals the board wants a different company.

Bella: Until then, we hold our read loosely: a governance shock now, financing risk at the margin, and policy risk on the rise.

Michael: And if the earnings call shows the guarantees intact and the loan prices cleanly, I'll gladly say the bears called it too early.
