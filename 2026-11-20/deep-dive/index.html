Deep dive: What a 900% Net Revenue Retention Actually Measures

AI Briefing · 2026-11-20 · follows today's briefing · written and read by AI from public sources

Anthropic has filed to list on Nasdaq under the ticker ANT. Its S-1 reports $16.3 billion in revenue for the first half of 2026.

Why today

Anthropic has filed to list on Nasdaq under the ticker ANT. Its S-1 reports $16.3 billion in revenue for the first half of 2026. A year earlier, the same period brought in $1.2 billion. Coverage is on its fourth day, and one figure keeps coming up. The filing says enterprise net revenue retention reached about 900% as of September 30, 2026. For comparison, The Stack puts Snowflake at 126% and Palantir at 157%. So 900% is not a slightly better result. It is a different kind of result. It also arrives as founders quote retention in almost every AI round, often on usage-priced revenue. To read a number like this well, you need to know exactly what it counts and what it leaves out. That's today's episode.

Plainly

Net revenue retention asks one question. Take the customers who were paying you at some earlier point. How much are those same customers paying you now? New customers are excluded. Churned customers stay in the group at zero. In the S-1's definition, the earlier point is the July to September quarter of 2025. The later point is the same quarter in 2026. You divide the group's current revenue by its base revenue. Two details matter. Second, Claude Pro and Max subscribers are excluded, because fixed-price plans don't expand. So this is mostly consumption revenue, billed by usage. Now a worked example. Picture a startup with 100 developer accounts in its base quarter. One account out of a hundred produced the whole headline. Push it further. Suppose 30 of the small accounts had quit entirely. The metric is weighted by dollars, not by customers. It can't tell you how many customers grew, how many left, or whether growth was broad or concentrated. It also rewards a small starting base. Anthropic's base quarter was when the company was far smaller, with many accounts on pilots and trials. Moving from pilot to production produces enormous ratios once. That move can't easily repeat, because next year's base is the production level. Usage pricing adds one more twist. There is no contract floor. Spend rises with tokens, and it can fall just as fast.

What the evidence shows

Here is what the filing establishes, as Anthropic reports it. The S-1 also gives customer tiers as of September 30, 2026. More than 11,900 customers had at least $100,000 of trailing twelve-month revenue. The Stack reports that figure was 950 a year earlier. More than 1,900 customers passed $1 million, and about 250 passed $10 million. That matters for the skeptical reading. Expansion this large isn't coming from one or two accounts. Concentration has also eased. No single customer exceeded 10% of revenue in the first nine months. TechCrunch adds that more than half of $100,000-plus customers now use both the platform and first-party apps like Claude Code. That share was about 30% at the end of 2025. Now the limits. These are self-reported operating metrics. The filing says they have not been independently verified by third parties. The S-1 does not give the number of customers in the base quarter. It does not disclose gross revenue retention, which shows how much revenue you keep before counting expansion. It does not disclose customer churn or median expansion. Anthropic attributes the number to, in the filing's words, "significant usage expansion as customers realize the value of deploying Claude across their organizations." That's a claim about cause, and it isn't tested. Pricing complicates things too. The Batch reports Anthropic's claim that Opus 5.5 cut cost per task by about 40% versus Opus 5. If that's right, usage grew even faster than spend. It also means spend depends on how many tasks customers run, which says little about lock-in. Finally, growth is slowing. Zvi Mowshowitz notes revenue grew 45% over three months in the third quarter. That is very fast, but slower than before.

How people read it

The same facts support different readings. The AGI-forward reading says the spend is real labor moving onto software. The tiers back that up: 250 customers paying more than $10 million a year is not a pilot pattern. TechCrunch reports that the top 5% of enterprise users who deployed Claude Code spent about 20 times more per month than average enterprise users without it. If agents keep taking on whole workflows, expansion continues, just at lower ratios. The AI-realist reading says 900% mostly measures a ramp that happens once, from tiny base-quarter usage to production. L.E.K. Consulting warns that under usage pricing, retention is "often inflated in the early months of a new customer ramp." Consumption with no contract floor can shrink in a budget cycle. It can also move to cheaper models. The Batch reports Kimi K3.1 Pro at $2 per million input tokens, with near-frontier agentic scores. The market reading falls between the two. Investors will price the trend, not the level. The number has to come down as the base grows. One more angle on distribution: HackerNoon notes that nearly half of revenue flows through AWS, Google Cloud and Azure. All three also sell competing models.

What it means for builders and investors

Our read is that the 900% reflects real demand. But it's the wrong tool for judging how durable that demand is. The filing shows broad adoption across thousands of six-figure customers. It doesn't show how much of that spend is committed, how much is volatile, or how much revenue is kept before expansion. We'd change that view if Anthropic disclosed gross retention and committed spend, and both held up. For builders and investors at seed and Series A, the lesson travels. Startups reselling or building on usage-priced inference will show the same pattern: small pilots, big production ramps, very large retention numbers. Some of it is lasting. Some is one customer's agent that hasn't hit its first budget review. We'd want to see the cohort table rather than one blended figure. We'd also want gross retention, customer-count retention, and the share of expansion from the top three accounts. One question worth exploring might be: if your three fastest-growing accounts went back to their spend from six months ago, what would retention be? A related one: how much of current usage is under a committed contract, and how much could be switched off next month?

What to watch

Three things would settle this. First, the trend in Anthropic's quarterly reports as a public company. The level will fall. How fast it falls, and where it levels off, will tell us whether expansion is broad or front-loaded. Second, whether Anthropic starts disclosing gross revenue retention, customer churn or committed spend. Lasting usage businesses tend to disclose them eventually, because the numbers help them. Third, what happens to spend per customer when cheaper open-weight models reach near-frontier agentic scores. If Moonshot releases the Kimi K3.1 Pro weights as expected, we may see within a few quarters whether customers optimize spend away.
