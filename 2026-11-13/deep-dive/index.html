Deep dive: When the Platform Buys the AI Point Product

AI Briefing · 2026-11-13 · follows today's briefing · written and read by AI from public sources

Toronto's Ada is merging into Dialpad in an all-share deal reportedly worth about a third of its 2021 unicorn price, and Dialpad's CEO will run the combined company. We look at why 2021-era AI customer-service apps are ending up inside platforms, and how liquidation preferences decide who gets paid when the exit price is below the last round.

Why today

Ada, the Toronto customer-service AI company, is merging with Dialpad, a California communications platform. The deal is expected to close on or around November 12. The Globe and Mail first reported it, and says the all-share deal values Ada at approximately $600 million CAD. Neither company has disclosed terms. In US dollars that is roughly $430 million, against the $1.2 billion Ada was worth after its 2021 Series C. The control signal is just as clear. Dialpad's CEO, Craig Walker, runs the combined company, and Ada's co-founder Mike Murchison becomes chief AI officer. Ada is not alone this year. Zendesk bought Forethought. So this is a good week to ask two questions. Why do AI customer-service point products keep ending up inside platforms? And when the price comes in below the last round, who actually gets paid?

Plainly

Start with the product. Ada began in 2016 building customer-service chatbots for other companies. When large language models arrived, it rebuilt around AI agents and cut staff along the way. Dialpad sells phones, contact-centre software and sales tools. In October 2025 it launched its own autonomous agent platform. That rate card is the core of the problem. A point product sells one capability, here the agent that answers the customer. A platform owns the phone line, the human agents, the call recordings and the billing relationship. Once the platform ships an agent priced near a dollar, the point product has to justify a premium on its own. Now the exit math. A liquidation preference is the investor's right to be repaid before common shareholders, mostly founders and employees, see anything. The standard term is 1x non-participating. The investor either takes back what they put in, or converts to common and takes their ownership share, whichever pays more. Here is a worked example, and it is hypothetical, because Ada's terms are not public. Say the 2021 investors put in $130 million at a $1.2 billion valuation. That buys them roughly a tenth of the company. So they take the preference and get $130 million back instead. That money comes off the top, ahead of everyone holding common. If most of it sits in preferences that rank ahead of common, common holders split what is left, after the preferred holders have recovered their money. One more wrinkle matters. This is an all-share deal. Nobody receives cash. Ada holders receive Dialpad stock, valued at whatever price Dialpad and Ada agreed for a private company. The headline number is only as real as that price.

What the evidence shows

Here is what is established. Both companies announced the combination themselves, in a Dialpad press release and in Ada's own posts. That confirms the structure, the expected close and the leadership: Walker as CEO, Murchison as chief AI officer. Dialpad's release says the combined company serves over 50,000 companies and that customers keep their current contracts and pricing. Those figures are self-reported. The 2021 round is well documented. Some reports name Tiger Global as lead, but The Logic and contemporary coverage name Spark. Dialpad's per-conversation prices come straight from its own rate card. Now what is reported but not confirmed. The $600 million CAD valuation comes from The Globe and Mail, and the companies have not confirmed it. The Globe and Mail reported that Ada's recent annual revenue is near US$50 million, which puts the $430 million deal at about 8.6 times revenue. It also reports that Ada cut half its marketing team in July and another 25 positions before the announcement. Treat those as press reports, not filings. And here is what nobody has shown. We don't know Ada's preference stack, its seniority order, or whether any round carried terms above 1x. We don't know the price per Dialpad share used in the exchange, so the $430 million is a paper figure. We don't know what Ada's revenue was growing at, or how much of it was LLM agent revenue rather than legacy chatbot contracts. One detail deserves attention. Dialpad's release says both companies are backed by investors including Andreessen Horowitz and Bessemer. Bessemer was in Ada's Series C. When the same investor sits on both sides of a stock-for-stock merger, its interest in the exchange ratio is mixed. That is a reason to read the terms carefully once they are known. It is not evidence of anything wrong. Finally, the market for this category is split in two.

How people read it

The same facts support several honest readings. The AGI-forward reading says agents are getting good enough to take over real labour budgets in support. In that world the winners are priced on the work they replace, not on software seats. That is why Sierra can carry a valuation near 100 times its reported ARR. On this reading, Ada did not fail because the category is weak. It fell behind faster, better-funded rivals in a market that is still forming. The AI-realist reading puts more weight on the plumbing. The agent itself is converging on a commodity built on the same frontier models. The hard parts are the phone network, the hand-off to a human, the customer record and the integration work. BetaKit also notes surveys suggesting many consumers would still rather speak to a human. If so, the agent is a feature of the contact centre, and the platform that owns the contact centre captures it. The market reading is plainer. Ada was priced at the 2021 peak. Reported revenue of roughly $50 million against a $430 million price is a normal software multiple, not a collapse. What changed is the multiple, not necessarily the business. The policy angle is narrower. A Canadian AI company with a Toronto base is now run from California. Reports say Dialpad plans to keep Ada's Canadian presence and most of its staff. How long that lasts is an open question.

What it means for builders and investors

Our read is that this deal is about distribution and control more than technology. Dialpad already had an agent and a price. What it bought was Ada's deployed agents, its enterprise accounts and its team, paid for in its own stock. For a seed or Series A company building an AI app on frontier models, the lesson is about where the wedge sits. If your customer's platform vendor can ship a near-equivalent at a dollar a conversation, your moat has to be something that vendor cannot easily rebuild. That could be proprietary workflow data, deep integration into systems of record, or an outcome guarantee the platform will not underwrite. For investors, the lesson is that headline exit value and common-holder value can diverge sharply. That matters for retention, because employees holding options can read a cap table too. One question worth exploring in diligence might be: if this company sold tomorrow to the platform it integrates with, at a third of its last price, what would common holders receive? We'd want to see that answer, plus the share of revenue that would survive the platform launching its own agent. We hold this provisionally. Disclosed terms showing a clean preference stack, or strong growth inside Dialpad, would soften it.

What to watch

Three things would settle this. First, the terms. If Dialpad raises money or files for an IPO, the price per share will show what Ada holders really received. Second, retention. Watch whether Ada's senior engineers and Murchison are still there a year after the November close, and whether Dialpad keeps its promise on third-party integrations. Those promises currently have no end date. Third, the premium end of the market. If Sierra and Decagon grow into their valuations, the agent layer can stand alone. If they end up sold to platforms as Fin and Cresta were, the platform reading wins. Their next rounds, or their absence, during 2027 will tell us.
