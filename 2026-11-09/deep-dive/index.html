Deep dive: How Deep Is a Model's Bias? Re-aligning Qwen Cheaply

AI Briefing · 2026-11-09 · follows today's briefing · written and read by AI from public sources

We cover what was shown, what wasn't, and what it means for trust, audits and sovereign buyers if political slant can be stripped off as cheaply as it can be put back.

Read it▶ Listen

Why today

Nous Research has released Arete-27B. It is Alibaba's open-weight Qwen3.8-27B, post-trained on about 52.8M tokens of supervised examples. Nous describes the goal as reinforcing Western values in a Chinese-trained model. Nous built an 803-prompt eval with seven categories, including freedom of expression, democracy and surveillance. On that eval, the base model took up the official Chinese state line 22% of the time. Arete did so 0.9% of the time. Nous says capability held steady. Its conclusion is blunt. Nous says the bias layer in Qwen is shallow, and that matters now because, in its words, "the vast majority of high quality open models are Chinese." One news report puts its downloads at 3 billion. Companies and governments are building on it while wondering who they are trusting. If the slant really is thin, the trust question changes. It stops being which lab trained the base model. It becomes who did the post-training, and who checked it.

Plainly

First, some vocabulary. Pretraining is the expensive part. A model reads a huge pile of text and learns language, facts and reasoning. Post-training is the cheaper finishing step. The lab shows the model examples of how it should answer, and its habits shift to match. Nous did only that second step. It generated about 50,000 sample answers using a stronger teacher model, and trained Qwen to answer that way. No reinforcement learning, no preference tuning, just worked examples. Here is the analogy. Picture a well-read employee who joined a company with strict media guidelines. They know the history. But on certain topics they have learned the house line, and they say it when asked. Now send that employee on a two-week course. Afterwards, asked about the same topics, they give the facts plainly. Their knowledge never changed. What changed is the habit sitting between what they know and what they say. That is Nous's claim about Qwen. The facts are in there, and a thin layer of trained habit decides whether they come out. Outside research backs the first half of that. A team that includes Neel Nanda introduced a testbed that uses censored Chinese language models as a natural setting for drawing out the secret knowledge those models hold back. Simple tricks, like few-shot prompting with truthful examples, get it out. But the analogy also shows the catch. A two-week course changes what someone says in the meeting room. It does not tell you what they say under pressure, in another language, or after another course pulls them back. Princeton researchers have studied exactly this. In their 2024 paper on shallow safety alignment, they found that a model's trained safety behaviour sits mostly in the first few words of its answer. In the paper's words, alignment adapts the model "primarily over only its very first few output tokens." Shallow behaviour is cheap to change. That cuts both ways.

What the evidence shows

Start with what is established, and how. Nous published the eval itself: all 803 prompts, the source articles, the required facts, the judge instructions and the scoring scripts. That is better than most values claims, because anyone can rerun it. Each prompt comes with 2 to 4 facts from dated sources, plus the official Chinese government wording. A judge model scores whether the answer states the facts and whether it adopts the official line. The judge is Grok 4.7, from xAI. On that setup, the propaganda rate fell from 22% to 0.9%. The average fact score rose from 2.32 to 2.98 out of 5. Freedom of expression moved most, from 60% propaganda to 1%. Other models make useful reference points. Independent work agrees the underlying bias is real. Aleph Alpha tested Qwen, DeepSeek and Kimi on 967 sensitive topics. Aleph Alpha sells European sovereign AI, so it has a stake in that finding. Its scoring was also done by its own AI system. Now, what is claimed but not shown. Every result here is self-reported, scored by a single AI judge, and run on Nous's hardware. Nous removed training prompts that overlapped the eval. But the training data covers the same seven categories. So this measures improvement on familiar ground, not on new topics. The post shows no tests on Chinese-language prompts, adversarial prompting, or further fine-tuning. Capability results come from two benchmarks. One is Hermes Bench, which Nous built itself: 65.60% against 65.77% for the base. The other is Terminal-Bench-2.1, at 70% against 67%. Nous itself says it does "not take credit for any seeming improvement" because of configuration differences. And "shallow" describes behaviour, not internals. No interpretability work was shown. Another lab, Lazarus AI, reports a similar drop on Qwen 3.5, from 84.2% to 4.1%. That is also self-reported.

How people read it

The facts support several readings. The AGI-forward reading says values are a steerable layer on top of general capability. If so, the base model becomes a commodity, and nationality matters less than the finishing work. Nous's result, plus the finding that suppressed knowledge stays in the weights, fits that story. The AI-realist reading takes the same evidence and stresses the other edge. If 52.8M tokens can strip a slant, a small amount of data can put one back. The 2023 Princeton work showed that 10 adversarial examples, costing under $0.20, broke GPT-3.5 Turbo's safety guardrails. Unlearning research presented at NeurIPS 2025 found that removed knowledge returned after fine-tuning on unrelated data. Thin means cheap to fix, and cheap to undo. The realist would also want more than one AI judge before trusting a values claim. Studies of AI judges document position bias and a preference for their own style of answer. The policy reading is different again. A procurement officer worried about Chinese-origin models is not only worried about talking points. Hidden triggers and data provenance are invisible to any behavioural eval, however good. Origin rules may survive this result. Then there is the market reading. Nous builds and brands open models. A clean, well-documented re-alignment is good marketing for its post-training skill. That doesn't make the result wrong. It does mean the most important number is still waiting on an outside replication.

What it means for builders and investors

Our read is that this moves trust away from the base model, but only partly. On the evidence shown, slant in what the model says looks cheap to change. Serving cost should not move: the weights stay the same size and the architecture is unchanged. A buyer who can tolerate Chinese-origin pretraining gets a strong open model at base-model inference prices, with the slant reduced. That creates a wedge for companies selling audited fine-tunes and independent values evals to regulated or sovereign buyers. Ongoing monitoring may be the bigger opportunity, because thin alignment can drift. The exposure falls on any company whose only moat is that it de-biased an open model. Nous has now published the eval and the recipe. A competent team with 50M tokens of curated data can repeat it. What lasts is the audit trail, the independent judge, and evidence that the behaviour holds after customers fine-tune. One question worth exploring in diligence: if a customer fine-tunes your aligned model on their own ordinary data, what happens to your bias numbers, and who outside your company measures that?

What to watch

Three things would settle it. First, independent replication. Because the eval is public, an outside group could rerun it with a different judge model, on Chinese-language prompts and topics outside the seven categories. Second, a robustness test. Take Arete, fine-tune it on harmless business data, and measure again. If the propaganda rate climbs back toward 22%, thin means fragile, not fixed. Third, the policy response. We'd want to see whether procurement rules for sovereign and regulated buyers start accepting audited post-training, or keep ruling models out by origin. None of these has a date. The first two could appear within weeks, because the tools are already public.
