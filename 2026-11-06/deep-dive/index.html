Deep dive: Claude and the vault: what an AI did in the lab

AI Briefing · 2026-11-06 · follows today's briefing · written and read by AI from public sources

Anthropic says Claude worked at every step of the first 3D structure of a prokaryotic vault particle, and Nature published AutoDNA, an AI-run molecular biology lab from Shanghai Jiao Tong University, in the same week. We go through which steps the model actually did, why a structure can be checked when the speed-ups can't, and where a seed or Series A company could own part of the AI-in-the-lab workflow.

Why today

On November 4, Anthropic's biology research lab published a note claiming the first 3D structure of a vault particle from a prokaryote, a simple single-celled organism like a bacterium. Vaults are huge, hollow barrels of protein found in cells from slime molds to humans. They were discovered in 1986, and nobody knows what they do. Anthropic says Claude worked at every step: searching protein databases, designing DNA, tuning the purification and processing the microscope data. It reports that purification fell from three days to about 90 minutes. It says a first structural model was ready in 34 hours. Anthropic estimates that would normally have taken weeks. The same day, Nature published AutoDNA, a lab from Shanghai Jiao Tong University where AI coordinates more than 20 types of instruments. Two very different claims about AI doing lab work came out together. So it's a good moment to sort out which parts can be checked, and which parts are a lab grading its own homework.

Plainly

Start with the object. A vault looks like a ribbed barrel with domed caps, built from many copies of one protein locked together. The human version uses 78 copies. Anthropic's version comes from a bacteriophage, a virus that infects bacteria, so the vault gets built inside a bacterial cell. Its protein, which the team calls L05, assembles into 96 copies, 48 in each half. To see the shape, the team used cryo-electron microscopy. Here is the analogy. Imagine working out the exact shape of a paper lantern when all you have is thousands of faint, grainy photos. Each photo shows an identical lantern frozen at a random angle. No single photo shows much. Software works out each photo's angle and averages them into one sharp 3D shape, called a map. Then someone builds an atomic model, threading the protein chain through that map like fitting a wire skeleton inside a plaster cast. Now, who did what. By Anthropic's account, Claude searched about 15 billion predicted proteins for vault relatives. It turned the chosen sequences into DNA designs ready to order. The team screened 44 candidates in a test-tube expression system. In living E. coli, 15 of the 24 selected proteins came out soluble. The purification story is the interesting part. Claude first argued against one standard separation step, and the team ran it anyway. Claude then suggested loading raw cell extract straight onto the column. The researchers hesitated, and it worked. For the microscope data, Claude set up and ran RELION, an open-source processing software, inside what Anthropic calls an internal multi-agent harness. Researchers steered the analysis. So the model planned the work and operated software. It never touched the samples. The bacteria, the microscope and the people at the bench produced the physical data. Anthropic's own phrase for the arrangement is "Under our direction." That describes a capable research assistant inside a skilled team, not an autonomous scientist.

What the evidence shows

Here is what makes this kind of claim stronger than most AI-for-science announcements. A 3D structure is an artifact, not an opinion. Once the map and model are released, any structural biologist can download them, refit the chains and see whether the model really matches the data. The field has done this for decades. The rat liver vault, solved by X-ray crystallography in 2009 at 3.5 angstroms, sits in the public Protein Data Bank with its 78 chains. That gives a reference for comparing the new particle's architecture. Now, what is missing today. Anthropic says it will release the structures and data soon. We found no database accession numbers. The preprint has been submitted to bioRxiv but was not yet live when the note came out. Nothing is peer reviewed. The note itself does not state the map's resolution. One outlet that covered it pointed out the same gap, along with the missing validation statistics. Anthropic also reports a small molecule in a pocket of each protein copy that it has not identified. That candour helps. The speed claims are weaker. The three-day purification is described as what's conventional, and the weeks for a model are Anthropic's own estimate. There was no comparison arm, meaning no matched team doing the same work without Claude. The one outside voice, the vault's co-discoverer Leonard Rome, was brought on by Anthropic as an adviser. AutoDNA is a different kind of evidence. It is peer reviewed in Nature and reports measured numbers. According to a 36Kr report on the paper, AutoDNA matched the enzymatic DNA synthesis yields optimized by human experts. Across more than 11,000 hardware steps in the project, it completed an end-to-end DNA data-storage cycle and recovered the original digital content exactly. It also hit 14 hardware errors. Six of those needed a human. Humans still chose the experiments and set up the platform.

How people read it

The same facts support different readings. The AGI-forward reading says the important part is breadth. One model contributed to literature search, DNA design, wet-lab protocol choices and image processing, and the whole project took under four months. On this view, the purification suggestion is the tell. The model argued with experts, proposed something unusual and turned out to be right. Pair that with a lab like AutoDNA running the instruments, and the loop starts to close. The AI-realist reading points out that every step Claude touched already had mature tools. RELION pipelines are well established. A barrel made of 96 identical copies gives the averaging step a lot of symmetry to work with. On this reading, the discovery came from a smart database search followed by ordinary good bench work, and the speed-ups are anecdotes with no control. AutoDNA's own authors note that fully automated work is still mostly standardised tests. The market reading looks at incentives. Anthropic's own life sciences team ran the search with Claude Science, which one report calls the company's setup for applying its AI models to scientific research, so these notes are the company describing work done with its own tools. That doesn't make them wrong. It means the checkable artifact matters more than the stopwatch. There is also a policy angle. AutoDNA comes from a Chinese university, and AI that operates biology instruments raises questions about who can intervene, and when, during an automated run.

What it means for builders and investors

Our read: the structure is probably real and will probably hold up once the data are public. We discount the speed-ups until someone measures them against a fair baseline. For builders, the uncomfortable lesson is about where value sits. Protocol planning and running analysis software are exactly what frontier labs are now demonstrating in-house. A startup whose product is an agent wrapped around RELION, or a protocol-planning chat window, looks exposed. The harder layers look more defensible. Instrument integration, error recovery, sample handling and proprietary experimental data are all physical and slow to copy. AutoDNA's error log makes the point: six of 14 failures still needed a person. Cost to serve is the open question. Neither source prices a run, and the expensive parts are likely still microscope time, reagents and skilled staff rather than tokens. One question worth exploring in diligence might be this. What share of experiments run end to end with no human touch, and what is the cost per successful result? We'd want that measured against a human-run baseline, the same way, on the same tasks. A company that can answer with its own logs has something. A company that cites a vendor's announcement probably does not.

What to watch

Three things would settle this. First, the preprint going live with the map's resolution and standard validation statistics. Second, the map and model appearing in the public structure databases, where independent groups can refit and challenge them. Anthropic says that release is coming soon. Third, an outside lab reproducing the 90-minute purification, or identifying the mystery molecule in the pocket. That would turn a self-reported speed-up into a method other people use. For AutoDNA, we'd watch for cost per experiment and for results on messier, non-standard tasks. We'd change our view if the released data show a weaker map than the note implies, or if other labs can't reproduce the method.
