Deep dive: Export Controls Follow the Renter: Capping China's Cloud Compute

AI Briefing · 2026-11-19 · follows today's briefing · written and read by AI from public sources

A new Commerce Department rule requires a US licence before overseas data centres can rent more than a capped amount of advanced AI compute to Chinese-headquartered firms. It also adds licence requirements for Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia and Brazil, and closes a foundry loophole. We cover how the cap works, what it means in training runs, and what enforcement would actually need to check.

Why today

On Monday, the Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security released a 132-page interim final rule. Its title is plain: Addressing Remote Access to Advanced Semiconductors. It is due in the Federal Register on November 19 and takes effect on publication. Comments are taken for 60 days afterwards, so the rule applies before the comment period ends. The core move is new. Until now, US export controls followed the chip: where it shipped and who bought it. This rule follows the renter. A data centre outside the United States that rents more than a set amount of compute to a Chinese-headquartered company now needs a US licence. Five countries also need licences for advanced chips: Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia and Brazil. The timing is not subtle. Days earlier, a former OpenAI engineer published analysis suggesting that leading Chinese open-weight models were started on Nvidia Blackwell systems. Those are chips China is not allowed to buy. Beijing has already promised countermeasures.

Plainly

Start with the gap the rule closes. Selling a Blackwell server to a company in Shenzhen is banned. Renting that same server by the hour from a data centre in Johor, Malaysia was largely unregulated. The chip never entered China. The training run did. Think of it like car hire. The old rules controlled who could buy a car. Anyone could still rent one. The new rule makes the rental agency check who is driving. It also caps how many cars one restricted customer can hire without permission. The unit of measure is total processing performance, or TPP. Commerce uses it to compare chips across generations: roughly, raw throughput weighted by numeric precision. The cap is more than 12 million aggregate TPP for any one covered customer, at a single data centre or across linked ones. Chris McGuire, a former National Security Council official now at the Council on Foreign Relations, puts that at roughly 200 Blackwells or about 800 H100s. Now a worked example. DeepSeek reported that its V3 model took 2.788M H800 GPU hours to train. The H800 is a Hopper chip made for China. Its compute is close to an H100's, with slower interconnect. Spread over 800 chips, that comes to about 3,500 hours. That is roughly five months of continuous running at perfect efficiency, which no real cluster reaches. And that was a model from late 2024. In McGuire's words, frontier work takes "thousands, tens of thousands, maybe hundreds of thousands of chips." So the cap leaves room for fine-tuning, evaluation and serving a modest product. It rules out a frontier training run at a single provider. There is an engineering wrinkle. The cap applies per data centre, or per group of linked data centres. Linked means facilities sharing resources and workloads as a unified whole. Training needs fast links between chips, so splitting a run across unrelated sites hurts badly. Inference splits easily. So the cap bites hardest on training and least on serving.

What the evidence shows

What is established comes from the rule itself, as summarised by the law firms WilmerHale and Pillsbury and reported by TechCrunch. Covered customers are companies headquartered in Macau or a D:5 country, the US arms-embargo list that includes China, Russia and Iran. The test also catches companies whose ultimate parent is headquartered there. Data centres already serving covered customers above the cap get 90 days to obtain a licence. The know-your-customer duty is the sharpest part. Exporters must get enough information about a foreign data centre's own customer checks. If the data centre refuses or cannot provide it, Pillsbury's summary says that counts as a red flag that presumptively gives the exporter knowledge. Exports to the five countries are exempt where the recipient is headquartered in the United States, subject to new reporting. On foundries, McGuire says any Chinese company making a chip at 14 nanometers or more advanced now needs a licence. That applies whatever the chip is claimed to be for. Now what is claimed but not shown. The 200 and 800 figures are McGuire's conversion, not the rule's wording. Retroactivity is contested. McGuire argues the rule treats past arrangements as violations, which could expose Megaspeed, the intermediary at the centre of recent smuggling reporting. The law firms describe it more narrowly: a clarification that an existing end-use rule already covered indirect and remote access. ChinaTalk's estimate that remote access supplies about two-fifths of China's AI compute comes from a best-guess breakdown, published in April, of the ratios between domestic, smuggled, legal foreign and remote-access compute. It is an estimate, not a measurement. The analysis of Chinese models is one analyst, Will DePue, reading hardware fingerprints in published weights. It is not peer reviewed. TechCrunch notes it does not show the models were trained entirely on Nvidia chips, or that any law was broken. The chips could have sat offshore, which is exactly the gap this rule targets. China's Ministry of Commerce opposes the rule and threatens countermeasures. Ars Technica notes Beijing has consistently accused the US of fabricating espionage claims to justify restrictions. That is a stated position, not evidence either way.

How people read it

The policy reading, from the export-control hawks, is that this is overdue. McGuire calls it "a good rule that actually moves the ball forward." He also names its biggest gap: "the rule doesn't apply inside the United States." His worry is that Chinese demand simply moves from Johor to Northern Virginia. He also expects smuggling to move to countries not on the list. The AI-realist reading puts the weight on enforcement. The rule depends on private operators checking ownership chains in places where Chinese tenants are their best customers. McGuire himself says compliance will be "really hard to verify." On this reading, Chinese labs keep their efficiency gains, and the cap mainly speeds up their move to Huawei hardware. The AGI-forward reading treats compute as the binding input for frontier models. On that view, even a leaky cap on training-scale rental protects a lead that compounds. Those readings differ on how much compute matters, not on the facts of the rule. The market reading is the most immediate. The New York Times reports the rule has rattled Southeast Asia, threatening a data centre boom fuelled by Chinese companies. Capacity built for that demand could sit idle or need relicensing, while US-headquartered operators gain an exemption. Beijing's reading, as Julian Gewirtz of Columbia frames its likely playbook, is to threaten retaliation and seek concessions at leader level.

What it means for builders and investors

We read this as a power story. Control of compute now follows the renter, not just the chip. That moves leverage toward licensed capacity in US-aligned hands, especially US-headquartered clouds, which keep their exemption in the five countries. For neoclouds in Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand and Indonesia, compliance starts to look like banking: tracing customers to their ultimate parent, and spotting resellers serving restricted firms. That is a real cost. For operators who do it well, it may also become a moat. For investors weighing Chinese cost-per-capability claims, the efficiency is real, but some of it ran on access that may now close. The weights already released stay available. The next generation is what is at risk. One question worth exploring in diligence of a GPU cloud or reseller: can they show their customer book traced to ultimate parents? And what share of revenue sits inside the 90-day licensing window? For an application company built on cheap Chinese open-weight models, we'd want to know whether its roadmap assumes the next releases arrive on the same schedule and at the same price. Our view would change if enforcement stays absent, or if the US gap closes quickly.

What to watch

Three things should settle how much this bites. First, the licensing window. Ninety days from publication lands in mid-February 2027. Whether licences get granted, denied or quietly ignored will show if the cap is real. Second, enforcement. A case against an intermediary like Megaspeed, built on the backward-looking reading, would tell operators the rule has teeth. No case would tell them the opposite. Third, the gaps and the politics. Watch whether Commerce extends the cap to data centres inside the United States, and whether leader-level talks soften the rule. In between, the next Chinese open-weight releases will carry fresh hardware fingerprints for analysts to read.
