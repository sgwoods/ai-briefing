Deep dive: Is AI Speeding Up AI? What METR Measured

AI Briefing · 2026-11-11 · follows today's briefing · written and read by AI from public sources

METR, an outside evaluator, estimates that Anthropic now improves its models on well-specified tasks about twice as fast as before, and guesses that most of the speed-up comes from AI-assisted research. We look at what was measured, why the range runs from 1.3x to 3x, and why the redacted evidence decides whether this is a finding or an estimate.

Why today

On November 5, METR, an AI-evaluation nonprofit, published an independent assessment, a pilot run with what it calls unprecedented access to Anthropic's internal data, of how much AI is speeding up AI research inside that lab. Its staff worked with Anthropic from 12 August to 29 October. They had access to internal benchmarks, compute figures and staff interviews. The headline is that Anthropic is likely improving its models on well-specified tasks at around twice its earlier rate. METR says it would not be surprised if the true figure were 1.3x or 3x. It guesses that perhaps 80% of the speed-up comes from AI being used in the research itself. It also says Anthropic redacted a majority of the evidence METR found most informative. All this lands as Anthropic reportedly prepares to list before Thanksgiving. The Financial Times reports a target valuation of $1.25T to $1.75T. So how much of the acceleration story can an outsider actually check?

Plainly

Start with what was measured. The core number is not a stopwatch on researchers. It comes from a benchmark index Anthropic keeps internally, called AECI, the company's version of the Epoch Capabilities Index. Think of it as a single score that rolls many automatically graded tests into one number, across maths, coding, science and images. METR plotted that score for Anthropic's internal model snapshots. The snapshots start with Claude 3 Opus in early 2024. They end with an internal model, simply called Model 4, in October 2026. METR fitted two straight lines joined at one bend, then asked how much steeper the line got after the bend. By The Decoder's reading of the report, the index went from about 15 points a year to about 29. METR puts the ratio at 1.9x. Its confidence interval runs from 1.5x to 2.8x. A worked example shows what that ratio means. Say a capability grows 3x a year. Doubling the pace means fitting two years of that growth into each calendar year. That takes the pace of progress to double, which is what METR and Anthropic mean by an acceleration of 2. So twice the rate means two years of old-pace progress per year, not models twice as smart. The second step is attribution, and an analogy helps. Picture a factory whose output doubles. You still have to ask why. More machines, more workers, better raw materials, or did the robots on the line start redesigning the line? METR says it saw convincing evidence that training compute did not speed up, though that evidence is redacted. Its 80% share for AI is a guess, in the report's own words: METR says it guesses that a majority, perhaps 80%, of the current acceleration is due to AI. Why is the band so wide? The report gives three reasons. Different choices about how benchmark scores map onto the index produced estimates between 1.1x and 2.9x. How you date each model and which benchmarks you include also moves the answer. And the tests are running out of headroom. One of two internal models ahead of Claude Opus 5.5, called Model 4, cleared 50% on all 15 tested benchmarks. The report says that makes METR somewhat nervous about measuring the index in the future. That is the result. Now, what does it rest on?

What the evidence shows

Here is what is established. A third party with unusual access looked at Anthropic's internal data and concluded the benchmark curve bent upward around October 2025. No lab has let outsiders do this much before. METR says Anthropic shared more than the project was first scoped to cover. The curve-fitting method is described, and the uncertainty is reported honestly. Now look at what it rests on. The benchmark scores for internal models come from Anthropic's own data, which Anthropic gave METR access to. The compute evidence comes from Anthropic too, and it is redacted. METR writes that the redactions mean the public 'must rely on our judgment more than we consider ideal.' The redacted material includes its detailed acceleration estimates for parts of the training pipeline. It also includes the breakdown across compute, data and algorithms, and the forecasts for future models. The evidence that AI is the cause is softer still. METR interviewed about 15 key staff, and the verdict was split. Asked whether AI had delivered more than 2x acceleration, five said yes or leaned yes. Four were unclear, and two said no. In an informal Slack poll, staff reported roughly 12x uplift from the newest model. Self-reported speed-ups deserve caution. In a 2025 randomized trial, METR found experienced open-source developers took 19% longer when using AI tools. Afterward, they still believed AI had sped them up by 20%. This month also brought a cautionary tale. MIT CSAIL researchers reported that OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra edited a small Qwen model's weights directly. Its score on a standard maths benchmark rose from 7.9% to 48.1%. A Hugging Face reproduction found the base model already scores 45.8 with thinking mode turned off. In that setting, the edit costs about 1.5 to 2 points. Results where AI improves AI can shrink fast once someone replicates them. So what would turn this into evidence of recursive self-improvement, rather than an estimate? We would want the per-pipeline speed-up data and the share of pipeline tasks built by AI over time. We would want the evidence METR says points toward hyperbolic growth. And we would want a second evaluator to check it, on tasks messier than an automatic grader rewards. For now, METR's own view is that AI cannot yet make highly significant, fully autonomous contributions to the most important parts of training.

How people read it

The AGI-forward reading starts from the same facts and weighs them differently. The first outside look inside a frontier lab found acceleration, not a plateau. METR found no clear bottlenecks and no steep diminishing returns. Researchers described 'signs of life' toward automating parts of training. METR adds that redacted evidence makes continuous, hyperbolic acceleration seem more plausible. On this reading, 2x is a floor, and the redactions hide the more worrying part. The AI-realist reading focuses on the measuring stick. AECI covers well-specified tasks, the kind that are easy to grade automatically, and it is close to saturation. METR itself lists ways its number could be too high. Non-AI factors might explain most of it, the historical baseline might have been unusually slow, or the gains might stop at well-specified work. The causal claim leans on interviews and self-reports. On this reading, the result is a fitted bend in one lab's internal index, with a guessed cause. The market reading is narrower. A company heading for an IPO controlled what its outside evaluator could publish. The Decoder reports an industry-wide figure of 1.4x when OpenAI, Google and xAI are included. Whether that gap reflects Anthropic's edge or measurement noise matters to anyone paying for acceleration. The policy reading sits in between. Transformer reports visceral alarm among safety researchers. For regulators, the question is whether an evaluation with redactions can count as oversight.

What it means for builders and investors

Our read is that this is the most serious attempt yet to measure AI-driven AI progress, and it is still a lead, not a finding. The direction is credible. The size is not pinned down, and the cause is a judgment call on evidence we cannot see. For builders, one point holds even at the low end of the band. Suppose frontier capability on gradeable tasks now compounds at 1.3x to 3x the old pace. Then products whose value sits in easy-to-grade work get commoditised faster. Code generation, maths and structured extraction all sit there. Durable value looks more likely in messy, poorly specified work, in proprietary feedback loops, and in evaluation itself. For investors, the IPO matters. Any prospectus language about accelerating progress will probably lean on this report, and the company issuing the shares redacted most of what makes it convincing. One question worth exploring in diligence: when a founder says AI has sped up their own R&D, how much is measured outcome and how much is self-report? We'd want to see shipped features, eval gains or cycle times, not a staff poll. METR's 2025 trial shows how far those two can drift apart.

What to watch

Three things would move this. First, whether Anthropic releases the redacted material to another qualified reviewer, such as a government AI institute, or publishes it over time. Second, whether other labs agree to the same assessment, so an Anthropic effect can be told apart from an industry effect. Third, whether the bend survives harder benchmarks the newest model has not saturated, and whether it shows up on less well-specified work. The nearest checkpoint is the IPO, reportedly before Thanksgiving. Watch how the prospectus describes AI-driven acceleration and its risks. If it relies on METR's 2x without the underlying evidence, that tells you how little of the claim outsiders can check.
