Deep dive: Sweden's Chatbot Block Is About Contracts, Not Countries

AI Briefing · 2026-10-29 · follows today's briefing · written and read by AI from public sources

Sweden's Government Offices blocked browser access to ChatGPT and Claude a week after the US ordered DeepSeek off federal civilian systems. The two moves rest on different grounds, and that difference tells AI companies selling to governments where the real gate is.

Why today

On Monday, October 26, Sweden's Government Offices blocked ChatGPT and Claude on staff work computers. This is the body that supports the prime minister's office and every ministry. Staff are being sent to Microsoft's Copilot instead. Six days earlier, the US Department of Homeland Security published an order removing DeepSeek from federal civilian systems. Put the two side by side and the headline writes itself: governments are walling off foreign AI. Is that true, though? The first reports we saw gave no reason for the Swedish move. The reporting since then, which traces back to Sveriges Radio, does give a reason, and the reason is not where the chatbot comes from. So today's question is narrower and more useful. When a government shuts out an AI tool, is it doing so over data handling, security, or politics? And what does each of those grounds mean for a company trying to sell to the public sector?

Plainly

Start with what Sweden actually did. It blocked the web versions of ChatGPT and Claude, the sites you open in a browser. It did not ban the models. A limited number of staff can still reach both through Intric, a Stockholm platform for building AI assistants. Intric says its platform runs on servers in Sweden, with no need for foreign cloud firms. Copilot was approved because, in the Government Offices' words, they had checked that tool and made it safer. And here's what makes it interesting. Microsoft has said Copilot will continue to be powered by OpenAI's latest models. Microsoft has also added Anthropic models to Microsoft 365 Copilot. So the same model families can stay in the building while the consumer front door gets locked. An analogy helps. Think of a ministry that bans staff from emailing documents to their personal Gmail. Nobody reads that as a ban on Google. It's a rule about which channel has a signed contract, an audit trail, and an admin who can change the settings. Sweden's block works the same way. The digitalisation director, Mikael Wettercrantz, wrote on the staff intranet that using AI services without formal contracts can have consequences for information security. He added that they had seen several examples of problematic use, covering both what was typed in and how the answers were handled. The US DeepSeek order works on a different principle. It was issued under the Federal Acquisition Supply Chain Security Act of 2018, a law that lets the government exclude a vendor it judges to be a supply chain risk. The issue there is who the vendor is. DHS argues that sensitive information from US government users could be reachable by Chinese authorities under Chinese law. The order covers DeepSeek's own products and third-party products built on DeepSeek. No contract fixes that, because the objection is to the vendor's legal home. So the two moves have a different mechanism. Sweden is saying, go through a contracted channel. The US is saying, not this vendor through any channel.

What the evidence shows

Here's what is established. The Swedish block took effect October 26 and covers browser access across the Government Offices. That comes from Sveriges Radio's reporting and the director's own intranet note, which several outlets quote. Sweden's Tax Agency and the National Board of Institutional Care already had similar blocks. The Enforcement Authority was preparing its own decision. An internal review at the Swedish Transport Administration earlier this year found roughly 10 AI services being used in what was described as a widespread and risky way. On the US side, the record is primary. The order was dated July 24 and published in the Federal Register on October 20. It applies to civilian agencies such as Treasury, State and Justice. The Congressional Research Service notes that the Defense Department and the Director of National Intelligence issued their own orders on September 8. DHS wrote that DeepSeek's models present multiple threat vectors. DeepSeek's own privacy policy says it stores users' personal data in the People's Republic of China. There's also a third data point. In February, the European Parliament's IT department turned off built-in AI features on lawmakers' devices. That covered Copilot along with ChatGPT and Claude. Its email said it could not guarantee the security of data uploaded to AI companies' servers. Now for what is claimed but not shown. We have no examples of what Swedish staff actually leaked, only that problems were seen. We don't know how many people are affected. We also don't know what Copilot settings were changed, or whether data stays in Sweden. On the US side, the order does not define what DeepSeek implementations means. Does a self-hosted copy of an open-weight DeepSeek model count? What about a fine-tune of one? The order doesn't say, and the CRS summary doesn't settle it. One more caution. The idea that US law lets American authorities demand chatbot user data was TechCrunch's framing of the Parliament story. The Parliament's own email didn't say it. So the evidence is lopsided. The contract-and-data-handling reading for Sweden is well supported. A political reading is something we'd be inferring, not something anyone in Sweden stated.

How people read it

Each reading starts from the same facts. The AGI-forward reading focuses on cost. If frontier models keep improving fast, a ministry that limits staff to one approved assistant gives up capability every quarter. On that view, blocks like this push usage into shadow AI on personal phones, which is exactly the leak they were meant to stop. The AI-realist reading says this is ordinary IT governance catching up. An audit found about 10 unmanaged services. The organisation then picked one under contract and blocked the rest. Nothing about it depends on what the models can do. The market reading points to who won. Microsoft already had the enterprise agreement, the admin controls and the security review on file. It ended up as the only approved tool, while serving OpenAI's models. Distribution and contracts beat model quality here, and the incumbent that holds the paperwork collects the seat. The policy reading has two layers. In Europe, data sovereignty is a rising theme, and Intric's pitch of Swedish servers with no foreign cloud is aimed straight at it. In the US, the DeepSeek order is explicitly about the vendor's country. China's Foreign Ministry called it politicizing technology and trade issues. Even so, the Swedish move doesn't support a reading of Europe banning American AI. The European Parliament blocked an American incumbent too. Sweden kept one. For now, the shared thread is controlled data flows, not who owns the vendor.

What it means for builders and investors

Our read is that this is a pattern, but mostly not the one the headline suggests. Two gates are forming. The first, and most common, is the contract. Is there a data processing agreement, admin control and an audit trail? Any vendor can clear that gate in principle, though incumbents already on the approved list have a head start measured in years. The second gate is origin. So far it applies to Chinese vendors in the US, and it can't be cleared with paperwork. For builders, that means public sector sales depend less on which model you use and more on the channel you sell through. A startup offering a thin wrapper over a consumer chatbot API is exposed to the first gate. A startup that runs on in-country infrastructure, or sells through an approved platform, may find this a tailwind. Intric is the local example. One diligence question worth exploring: if the company's main model provider were blocked for a given buyer tomorrow, how fast could it switch, and does its contract with that buyer survive the switch? We'd also want to see whether its open-weight dependencies trace back to a vendor that has been excluded.

What to watch

Three things would settle this. First, whether OpenAI or Anthropic sign formal agreements with Swedish government bodies, and whether the blocks then lift. If they do, it was about contracts. If they don't, the sovereignty reading gains ground. The Enforcement Authority's pending decision is the next data point. Second, how DHS treats self-hosted and fine-tuned DeepSeek models. That will show up in the exceptions agencies request and in any guidance clarifying DeepSeek implementations. Third, whether any European body excludes a US vendor on origin grounds rather than contract grounds. Nobody has done that yet. If one does, the picture changes, and we'd revise this view.

Steven's take

Trust is not a security position it turns out. FAAFO. The demand for state-approved or sovereign solutions will be determined by vendor trust, operator control and the relative secrecy of not just the content but the use of the harness, agents, tools and the way in which this use is used to improve artifacts or models that are in the control of the payer/users (government, companies, universities) or the control of the model or service provider. Note - this includes things ABOVE the frontier model and perhaps the frontier ends up commoditized completely in which case the third scaling law of learning above the model isolated from the overall frontier training and improving cycles becomes the defensible and secure layer. This would bode VERY well for companies building above.
