Deep dive: Proving AI ROI: Does Outside Counsel Spend Actually Fall?

AI Briefing · 2026-11-07 · the weekly flagship, two voices · follows today's briefing · written and read by AI from public sources

We compare those claims with independent invoice data and surveys of general counsel, and set out what it would take to prove that AI is replacing billable outside work.

Why today

Bella: Harvey, the legal AI company, published a post profiling how the legal teams at Bitfury, ICL Group and SUSE are using its platform to expand capacity. Two of them put a number on what every vertical AI company wants to claim: less money going to outside law firms.

Michael: And they're big numbers. Bitfury, a former technology provider turned investment firm, says it reduced its reliance on outside counsel by more than 50%. ICL Group, a specialty minerals company, says 20%.

Bella: That range is why we're giving it a full episode. Outside counsel spend is a real line on a company's profit and loss statement. If AI moves that line, a legal tool stops being a nice-to-have and becomes a budget a general counsel can defend to the CFO.

Michael: Here's my problem before we even start, Bella. These are three accounts Harvey chose to feature, on Harvey's own blog. The word they use is reliance, not spend. Nobody has shown an invoice.

Bella: Fair, and that's the question for the whole episode. What evidence would actually show AI replacing billable outside work? Invoices before and after, a change in the mix of matters, or survey estimates? Survey estimates are mostly what we have.

Michael: And it goes well beyond legal. Any vertical AI company selling against a cost line, whether that's contractors, agencies or consultants, has the same proof problem. The cost line is easy to see. What would have happened without the tool is not.

Bella: The timing helps. This year's independent spend reports are out, so we can set Harvey's stories against data that doesn't come from a vendor. First, let's be clear about what the claim actually is.

Plainly

Bella: Start with how it's supposed to work, because the logic is sound. A small in-house team often has the expertise for routine drafting and diligence but not the hours. So that work goes to a law firm, billed by the hour.

Michael: So the AI is standing in for junior associate hours at the firm.

Bella: That's the claim. According to Harvey's post, Bitfury's team now uses the tool to bring transaction documents roughly 80% of the way to a final draft. The outside firm then reviews the deal instead of building the work from scratch.

Bella: They do the same with data rooms during due diligence. The lawyers also use it for a first understanding of foreign regulations, which used to mean hiring local counsel every time a deal entered a new country.

Michael: I believe the workflow. As an engineer I'd expect exactly that split: the machine drafts, a human reviews. What I don't understand is what a 50% cut in reliance means as a measurement. Is it dollars, hours, or matters sent out?

Bella: The post doesn't say. It's an outcome with no unit attached. ICL's 20% is the same.

Michael: Then let's make it concrete, because I think the unit is where this falls apart.

Bella: Here's a worked example. Did AI cut its spend in half?

Michael: Depends what happened to the work.

Bella: Exactly. Suppose last year included a big acquisition and a lawsuit, and this year had neither. Spend would have fallen anyway, tool or no tool.

Bella: Now flip it. That's a real saving the invoice total hides completely.

Michael: So a raw comparison of invoices can mislead in either direction.

Bella: Yes. An honest measurement adjusts for the mix of matters. You compare like with like, deals against deals and disputes against disputes, before and after. Energy-efficiency firms have the same problem: a building's bill drops in a mild winter, so they adjust for weather before claiming the retrofit worked.

Michael: Good analogy. Here the weather is deal flow, litigation and regulatory surprises. But that means all the judgment sits in the estimate of what would have happened otherwise. Who makes that estimate?

Bella: In these stories, the customer does. SUSE, the open-source software company, says it saved up to £80,000 in external fees on one acquisition's due diligence.

Michael: Look at ICL's range. The top is nearly double the bottom. That tells you it's a guess at what a firm would have charged, not a number off an invoice.

Bella: I'd put it a bit more kindly, Michael. It's an estimate of what a firm would have bid, which legal ops teams do all the time. It's a real method. It just isn't a measured saving.

Michael: Agreed, and I'll concede that the single-matter version is the strongest evidence here. One defined deal with a known scope, done in-house. That's much closer to a controlled comparison than a reliance percentage.

Bella: There's a second subtlety. Capacity and spend are different things. SUSE reports nearly 1,500 hours saved over four months. ICL says the tool gave its core team the equivalent of an extra full-time lawyer.

Michael: And neither of those is outside spend. Hours saved inside the team only turn into dollars if you'd otherwise have hired someone or sent the work out. Bitfury says it would need at least three more lawyers without Harvey. That's a hire it imagines avoiding, not a cost it removed.

Bella: So, plainly: the mechanism is credible, the units are missing, and the savings are estimates against an imagined baseline. That's where the evidence has to start.

What the evidence shows

Bella: Let's separate what's established from what's claimed. Established: three named customers, Bitfury, ICL Group and SUSE, use Harvey on real matters and say so publicly. Companies rarely put their names to a vendor story they don't believe.

Michael: Established that they say it, yes. It's still self-reported, published by the vendor and chosen by the vendor. We don't know how many customers were asked and said no, or saw no change at all.

Bella: Harvey's longer report, The New Economics of In-House Legal, adds a little. It draws on a survey of 25 in-house legal teams and 22 in-depth interviews. Harvey commissioned it, and a research firm called RSGI ran it in June 2026; the report doesn't say whether the people in it were Harvey customers.

Michael: So it's a customer survey with a research firm's name on it. The headline findings are that 92% report faster turnaround and 65% saw measurable benefits within a month. Neither of those is a spend number.

Bella: Fair. Now the independent data. The best source is LexisNexis CounselLink's annual trends report. In its words, it's built on actual invoices paid to law firms for work completed in 2025.

Michael: That's the dataset I'd want. What does it show?

Bella: Not a collapse. Outside counsel fees kept rising across practice areas and regions. Big Law, about 100 firms, holds a 51.2% share of wallet. Alternative fee arrangements average just 8.3% of matters.

Michael: So in the invoice data the billable hour is fine, and the biggest firms hold the largest share. That's hard to square with drops of 20 to 50%, unless those drops sit in small teams doing routine work.

Bella: Which is exactly what the stories describe: routine drafting, first-pass diligence, local counsel for research. Much of that may sit with mid-sized firms. If routine work leaves first, Big Law's share could rise even while total spend falls.

Michael: Possible. But you've just built that theory to rescue the claim. Also, CounselLink covers invoices from 2025, and much of this adoption is more recent. The invoice data may simply be too early to show anything either way.

Bella: Agreed, timing cuts both ways. Now the surveys of general counsel. Thomson Reuters' 2026 State of the Corporate Law Department report found 36% of general counsel expected to increase outside counsel spend. Only 20% planned to cut it.

Michael: That's a survey of intentions, taken in late 2025. But no vendor ran it, and it points the other way.

Bella: CLOC's 2026 report, from a survey of leaders at 135 law departments, shows where things are moving. Only 37% now expect outside counsel spend to rise. A year earlier it was 58%.

Michael: I'll give you that one; it's a real shift in intent. But it could be budget pressure, not AI. CLOC also found that expected increases in inside spending fell from 65% to 47%. Everyone is tightening.

Bella: Fair point. If inside budgets are tightening too, it isn't clearly a story about moving work in-house. It may just be less money everywhere.

Michael: Then there's a survey published by the Association of Corporate Counsel. Of 657 in-house professionals, 59% reported no noticeable savings yet from their law firms' use of AI.

Bella: That measures something different, though. It's about firms passing on their own AI efficiencies. Harvey's claim is that clients do the work themselves.

Michael: True, and it actually helps Harvey's pitch. If firms won't pass savings on, clients have a reason to bring work in-house. But it still means none of the independent data shows the money moving yet.

Bella: So here's where the evidence leaves us. Established: the workflow is real, and customers believe it saves money on specific matters. Claimed but not shown: a measured fall in outside counsel invoices, adjusted for matter mix, at any customer.

How people read it

Bella: How do the different readings take these same facts? Start with the AGI-forward reading. On that view, Bitfury's 80% draft is an early snapshot of a steep curve. Models get better at drafting and diligence every year, so less and less work needs to go outside.

Michael: The strongest version of that is about the baseline. If a three-lawyer team can support global deals, the right comparison isn't last year's invoices. It's the larger team and the outside counsel the company never had to pay for.

Bella: And on that reading, invoice totals will understate the effect, because growing companies spend more on legal no matter what. The saving shows up as costs that never arrive.

Michael: The AI-realist reading is closer to mine. The independent invoice data shows fees rising and Big Law holding over half the wallet. The vendor data is three hand-picked accounts and a survey of 25 customers. Until those two meet, it's a marketing story with plausible mechanics.

Bella: A realist would also ask what the tool really replaces. Outside counsel isn't just hours. It's judgment, accountability, and a firm whose insurance stands behind the advice.

Michael: Which is what Bitfury describes: outside firms now review instead of drafting. That's real, but review is often expensive partner time. The cheap associate hours go first, so the dollar saving may be smaller than the hours suggest.

Bella: Then there's the market reading, which asks who keeps the savings. Law firms are adopting AI too. If they keep billing by the hour while their costs fall, the efficiency shows up in firm margins, not client invoices.

Michael: And alternative fees at 8.3% of matters suggest billing hasn't moved much. So clients have two routes: bring the work in-house with a tool, or push firms onto fixed fees. Both squeeze the billable hour, from different sides.

Bella: There's also a quieter liability angle. When an in-house team relies on a model's first draft, the company carries the risk of a mistake a firm used to carry. That cost appears in none of these savings estimates.

Michael: I agree with that. The readings disagree less about the facts than about the baseline. The forward reading compares against an imagined world, the realist against last year's invoices. They're measuring different things.

What it means for builders and investors

Bella: So, our read. We'd discount the 20 to 50% figures until a customer shows outside counsel invoices before and after, adjusted for matter mix. Three hand-picked accounts estimating their reliance is closer to a survey than a measurement.

Michael: But we still think outside counsel spend is the right line to sell against. It's real money, it sits on the profit and loss statement, and the CFO already tracks it.

Bella: For builders, there's a product hiding inside the proof problem. A company that can measure the shift, matching matters to invoices and estimating what work would have cost outside, may win the procurement conversation. Whoever audits the spend may end up owning the budget.

Michael: Which gives an edge to the e-billing and spend-management platforms that already hold the invoices. A seed-stage company would need integrations or a partnership to get at that data.

Bella: For investors, one question worth exploring in diligence might be this. Can any customer show outside counsel invoices for the same matter types, for 12 months before and 12 months after adoption?

Michael: And a follow-up we'd want answered: do renewals actually rest on that measured number, or on hours saved and user satisfaction? If it's satisfaction, the tool is priced as productivity software, whatever the pitch deck says.

Bella: That test applies far beyond legal. Any vertical AI company claiming to replace contractors, agencies or consultants is selling against a visible cost and an invisible counterfactual.

Michael: Here's where we still disagree, Bella. I think capacity gains without a falling invoice mean buyers are paying for an easier workload, not lower costs. That's a smaller and more crowded market.

Bella: I read it more generously. CLOC's drop from 58% to 37% expecting higher outside spend looks like early evidence that intent is changing. And single-matter estimates like SUSE's and ICL's are the kind legal ops teams actually use to approve budgets.

Michael: Intent isn't spend. But I'll grant that single-matter evidence beats the headline percentages.

Bella: Then we agree on the hierarchy. Invoices adjusted for matter mix beat single-matter estimates. Those beat reliance percentages, and reliance percentages beat satisfaction surveys.

What to watch

Bella: What would settle it? First, a customer, or better an independent auditor, publishing outside counsel invoices before and after adoption, broken out by matter type. One clean case would be worth more than a stack of testimonials.

Michael: Second, the next CounselLink trends report. If it keeps this year's timing, it should land next spring and cover invoices paid in 2026. Watch whether routine-matter hours fall, and whether Big Law's 51.2% share keeps climbing.

Bella: Third, the next round of general counsel surveys from CLOC and Thomson Reuters. If fewer expect higher outside spend while inside budgets hold, that looks like work moving in-house. If both fall together, it's just belt-tightening.

Michael: And one more from me: pricing. If legal AI vendors start charging a share of the outside spend they save, they'd have to measure it. That would show they believe their own numbers.

Bella: What would change our view? Matter-adjusted invoices showing even a 20% drop would make us take the headline seriously. Routine-matter hours at firms rising despite adoption would make us doubt it.

Michael: Until then, it's a plausible mechanism with estimated savings. Worth tracking, not yet worth underwriting.

Steven's take

Super powering experts is what AI does - consultants are the opposite path.
