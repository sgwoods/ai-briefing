AI Briefing · special edition · November 12

AI Briefing · 2026-11-12 · special edition · written and read by AI from public sources

Jensen Huang has resigned as Nvidia's CEO, president and director with immediate effect, and CFO Colette Kress is interim CEO while the board searches for a permanent one. We separate what is confirmed from what is only reported, explain why losing a founder who made so many decisions himself matters more for new deals than for chips already shipping, and look at what that means for AI companies being built now.

Why today

Nvidia's board has accepted the resignation of Jensen Huang as president, chief executive and director, effective immediately. He co-founded the company in 1993 and led it for 33 years. Chief financial officer Colette Kress becomes interim CEO and, according to Nvidia, will continue to serve as CFO until a permanent successor is named. Harvey C. Jones, a director since 1993, becomes the board's first-ever chair. The company gave no reason. The news coverage gives the context. One reportedly involved a woman US officials suspect of working for Chinese intelligence. Ars Technica reported that the FBI is investigating. The stock fell more than 12% in early trading. The timing matters. Nvidia reports quarterly results on November 17. Nvidia is reportedly set to invest $10 billion in Anthropic immediately following its IPO, which is expected next week. So the question this week is simple. Does a change at the top of the company that sells the AI industry's picks and shovels change how the shovels get handed out?

Plainly

The cleanest way to think about this comes from networking. A router has a data plane and a control plane. The data plane forwards packets along routes that already exist. The control plane decides which routes exist at all. If the control plane fails, traffic keeps flowing for a while on the old routes. What stops is new routing. Nvidia's data plane is the physical supply chain. Nvidia relies on key manufacturing partners in Taiwan such as TSMC, Foxconn and Wistron. Those contracts were signed months or years ago. That is why the board's statement stresses that operations, customer commitments and the product roadmap should continue without interruption. Nvidia's control plane is something else. It is which companies Nvidia puts money into, like the billions it promised Anthropic in the IPO, and whether its customer commitments really continue without interruption. Huang told podcaster Lex Fridman that he has more than 60 direct reports and holds no one-on-one meetings with them, according to Business Insider. That flat design makes a company fast when the hub is present. It also means a lot of judgment, relationships and deal memory lived in one head. Here is a worked example. Picture a Series A company that trains models on rented GPUs from a smaller cloud provider. That provider may depend on Nvidia for its supply. It may also depend on Nvidia's money, as an investor or a backstop. The startup's data plane, its current cluster, is fine this week. Its control plane is the next expansion, the renewal price and the provider's next funding round. That runs through decisions an interim, finance-led team now has to make. The chips keep shipping. The open question is whether the next deal gets made on the same terms.

What the evidence shows

Here is what is established, and how we know it. Nvidia's own announcement and its SEC filing confirm the resignation, the interim role for Kress and the appointment of Jones as chair. The filing says the board accepted the resignation on November 11. It gives no reason and says nothing about severance or equity. The board says it will consider internal and external candidates for the permanent job. That is the board's own assessment, made while it says its independent investigation is still running. The Wall Street Journal reported that the board was preparing to push Huang out. The New York Times reported the two relationships. Bloomberg reported that an outside firm is helping with the search. The FBI investigation comes from Ars Technica's reporting, and its scope is not stated in the material we have. Now the claims that go beyond the evidence. Gary Marcus, a long-time sceptic of the AI build-out, called Huang "the ringmaster of all the insane circular financing" and wrote that "all that might collapse." That is a prediction, not a finding. Circular financing here means Nvidia investing in customers who then buy Nvidia chips. Nvidia has invested at least $30 billion in OpenAI, and Anthropic said in an amended prospectus that Nvidia has agreed to buy another $10 billion of its Class N common stock immediately following the offering. Nothing published so far says either commitment is in doubt. On succession, analysts float internal names. Most often it is Ian Buck, a key architect of CUDA who now runs the hyperscale business. Prediction-market odds are not evidence of what the board will do. Gil Luria of D.A. Davidson said Nvidia does not have a succession plan in place. That is an analyst's view, not a disclosure. And a Taiwan supplier executive told Reuters the new CEO "may not be as aggressive as Jensen." That is one anonymous voice. It is a useful signal, not a measurement.

How people read it

The AGI-forward reading says demand for compute sets the pace, not any single executive. Labs need more training and inference capacity whoever runs Nvidia. Chip roadmaps are fixed years ahead. On this reading, the selloff is a governance discount that fades once a credible permanent CEO is named. Some early trading fits it. AMD and Broadcom rose in pre-market trading, which suggests investors saw a company problem rather than a demand problem. The AI-realist reading points to the control plane. Nvidia's role went beyond selling chips. It helped finance its own customers, and Huang was the dealmaker. An interim CEO who is also the CFO has every reason to look hard at large equity commitments before signing more. Pressure on that side would hit the most leveraged parts of the build-out first. Watch the smaller clouds and the labs that rely on vendor money. The market reading is about competitors. AMD shares were up nearly 17% since Monday. Customers who wanted a second source now have a board-level reason to push for one. That said, switching away from CUDA still costs real engineering time. The policy reading may matter most over time. A reported China-linked intelligence concern, an FBI inquiry and a House committee chair saying Huang is willing to testify all invite scrutiny of export controls. Tighter rules would reshape where Nvidia can sell, whoever runs it.

What it means for builders and investors

Our read: this is a governance shock, not yet a supply shock. Hardware already contracted will keep arriving. What has changed is how certain the next round of terms is: allocation, pricing and vendor financing. For builders, the exposure is indirect and easy to miss. Few seed or Series A companies buy from Nvidia directly. Many rent from providers whose capacity, and sometimes whose balance sheet, rests on Nvidia's goodwill. A company whose model works only at today's GPU price, from one provider, on one software stack, carries more risk this week than last. Nothing about its product changed. Its supply chain just lost the person who made the calls. One question worth exploring in diligence might be this. If your compute provider's terms moved by a meaningful amount next year, what would happen to your gross margin and your runway? A good answer names a second provider, has tested running off CUDA, or shows margins that hold up when the price moves. What would change our view is evidence of the control plane working without Huang. That means an interim team reaffirming the big commitments and signing new ones on time.

What to watch

Three things would settle this. First, the November 17 earnings call. Listen for whether Kress reaffirms guidance and speaks plainly about the investment commitments, or goes quiet on them. Second, the Anthropic IPO, expected on November 19. If Nvidia's reported $10B purchase goes through on schedule, the vendor-financing worry eases. If it is delayed or cut, it sharpens. Third, the choice of a permanent CEO, and its timing. An insider with deep customer and CUDA ties would signal continuity. A long search, or findings from the board's investigation or Congress, would keep the discount in place for months.
